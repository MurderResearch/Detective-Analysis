# Arsène Lupin Intervenes — Deducibility Analysis

## Basic Information
- Author: Maurice Leblanc
- Year: 1929
- Type: Short story collection (8 stories + preface and afterword)
- Detective: Jim Barnett — Arsène Lupin himself
- Core mystery types: whodunit / howdunit / false appearance deconstruction (alibi trick)

---

## Overall Difficulty Rating

| Indicator | Rating | Description |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Average convergence point approximately 78% — the key mechanism in each story is buried deep in the latter portion, making it nearly impossible for readers to deduce the truth before it is revealed |
| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Leblanc habitually employs a "credible false suspect" strategy: every case features a surface-plausible direction of suspicion, and physical evidence often supports the misleading direction |

---

## Synopsis

*Arsène Lupin Intervenes* is a late-period short story collection by Leblanc, framing Lupin's true identity within the conceit of a detective agency. The book is designed around a clever meta-puzzle: who exactly is the private detective "Jim Barnett"? From the very preface, he is half-revealed — his reasoning, his methods, his teasing disdain for the police all point unmistakably toward the name that gave the entire Paris police force headaches: Arsène Lupin.

Barnett forms an odd partnership with Inspector Béchoux: Béchoux brings the cases, Barnett solves the mysteries, and after each case is closed, Barnett always finds some roundabout way to collect his "fee" — sometimes a pearl necklace, sometimes a royal love letter, sometimes a piece of antique furniture, sometimes a charming ex-wife.

The collection contains eight stories in total. This analysis focuses on the four with the most intricately designed mystery mechanics:

## Selected Story Analyses

### Story One: Drops That Trickle Away ("Drops That Trickle Away," Chapter Two)

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery types: howdunit + hidden method
- Victim: Baroness Renée (targeted by her husband's revenge)

#### Overall Difficulty Rating

| Indicator | Rating | Description |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Average convergence point approximately 72% — the husband can be suspected early (25%), but the mechanism itself (pearls hidden in a drainpipe) is nearly impossible to deduce |
| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐ | The plumber's flight is the biggest red herring, but the clues are laid out fairly enough |

#### Truth Breakdown

##### Truth One: Where Are the Pearls? (howdunit)
- **Clue convergence point: approximately 70%**
- Answer: The Baron had previously hired a plumber to alter the bathroom drainpipe, creating a hidden "pocket" with a hole into which a rubber stopper could be inserted. He waited until night, removed the real pearls from the safe, placed them inside the pipe, and substituted fake pearls. On his deathbed, he insisted his wife pour water from the tap, sending the pearls flowing away — this was the final punishment he had meticulously engineered.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | A plumber "repairs" the Baron's bathroom tap, then immediately flees to Brussels | ~15% | Surface-level red herring, but in fact the core of the mechanism: the plumber was hired to modify the drainpipe |
| 2 | The Baron is gravely ill but not entirely bedridden; he has long known the safe combination (years of observation) | ~20% | Establishes that the husband was capable of carrying out the substitution |
| 3 | A replica of the necklace was commissioned by the Baron four years earlier | ~35% | Reveals the plan was long premeditated, not a spontaneous act |
| 4 | On his deathbed, the Baron insists on water "poured from the tap" rather than from a pitcher | ~80% | **Key clue**: his irrational insistence implies that turning on the tap has special consequences |
| 5 | The Baron murmurs, "Do you hear them… they are trickling away… disappearing…" | ~85% | Convergence point — "trickling" unmistakably points to pearls flowing down the drainpipe; the mechanism fully surfaces here |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | The display cabinet is overturned and ceramics shattered, yet there are no signs of the safe being forced open | ~18% | Indicates the intruder knew the combination, narrowing the field to "family member or intimate acquaintance" |
| 2 | The Baron is wealthy, ruling out theft motivated by financial need | ~30% | Implies revenge rather than greed is the motive |
| 3 | The Baroness's long-running misconduct and the Baron's accumulated resentment | ~22% | Provides motivational background, lending credibility to the hypothesis of a husband scheming for revenge |

##### Red Herrings

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Misleading Direction |
|---|-------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | The plumber flees to Antwerp and spends money freely | ~18% | Leads readers to believe the plumber is the thief or even a co-conspirator |
| 2 | An old tool (an awl) and candle residue at the scene | ~16% | Resembles evidence left by an outside burglar; in fact these were left behind by the Baron |

##### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

At around 15% (the plumber repairs the washbasin, then flees), an attentive reader should notice the suspicious coincidence between the plumbing work and the theft. By 25% (Barnett hints the husband may be a suspect), a perceptive reader can begin hypothesizing that the Baron is behind everything. However, the question of "where are the pearls" receives no real answer until 80%, when the dying Baron insists on using the tap; the 85% "trickling" murmur is the true convergence point. The high difficulty of this mystery lies in the fact that the mechanism's physical design — the modified drainpipe — requires the reader to have some prior imagination of plumbing structure, and Leblanc provides almost no physical foreshadowing of that detail.

##### Additional Notes

The most brilliant aspect of this story is the "perfect silent revenge": the Baron's scheme ensures that his wife is the one who turns on the tap, causing the pearls to flow away, while he dies carrying that knowledge with him. This design — making the victim personally execute her own punishment — carries a profound moral weight. Barnett/Lupin ultimately leverages this secret, trading the necklace for the will — a second act of extortion against the Baroness, and a perfect demonstration of Lupin's consistent philosophy of "committing theft in the name of justice."

### Story Two: The Royal Love Letter ("The Royal Love Letter," Chapter Three)

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery types: whodunit + fake alibi
- Victim: Bookseller Vaucherel, stabbed to death

#### Overall Difficulty Rating

| Indicator | Rating | Description |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Average convergence point approximately 80% — Leboc's painted-window trick is nearly impossible to guess |
| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Multiple suspicious figures (the three Gautru brothers vs. Leboc), physical evidence pointing to the innocent, and narration deliberately crafting the illusion that "the villainous-looking are innocent, the respectable-looking are guilty" |

#### Truth Breakdown

##### Truth One: Who Is the Killer? (whodunit)
- **Clue convergence point: approximately 82%**
- Answer: The retired official Leboc killed the bookseller Vaucherel. He had previously hired someone to paint a full-length portrait of himself "smoking at the window," mounted within the window frame, creating a perfect alibi — five witnesses believed he had been at home the entire afternoon.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | A full-length portrait hangs in the victim Vaucherel's room (amateur style but a strong likeness) | ~38% | **Most important foreshadowing**: a portrait of Leboc in the same style must also exist, implying the same painter also painted Leboc |
| 2 | Barnett observes Leboc quietly pinning a visiting card to the floor with his boot, then secretly pocketing it when he leaves | ~45% | Reveals Leboc deliberately concealing his connection to Élisabeth Löwenthal — he knows that card's existence is incriminating |
| 3 | Four hobnails on the boot sole are arranged in a diamond pattern, exactly matching four holes in the visiting card | ~50% | Barnett deduces from this that the card was in Leboc's pocket: in trying to hide the connection, Leboc has revealed a far more damning detail |
| 4 | Leboc places the book of love letters into the Gautru family's fireplace (witnessed directly by Barnett) | ~70% | Confirms Leboc is the real culprit and that he is deliberately framing the Gautru brothers |
| 5 | Barnett enters Leboc's home in advance and re-hangs the rolled-up canvas in the window | ~85% | Convergence point — the painted-window trick is forced into the open; all clues converge here |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | The "Leboc" seen by witnesses at the window never moved and never responded to calls | ~65% | In hindsight, "a person who never moves" ought to have raised suspicion |
| 2 | Leboc's composure and total lack of agitation throughout | Throughout | A truly guilty and well-prepared person is often the one who behaves most naturally and "of course" |

##### Red Herrings

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Misleading Direction |
|---|-------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | The villainous appearance and suspicious movements of the three Gautru brothers | ~30% | Appearance bias steers readers to treat them as natural suspects |
| 2 | The victim Vaucherel cries out "Leboc! Leboc!" as he dies | ~40% | Superficially seems like an accusation, but Leboc's camp interprets it as the victim calling to a neighbor for help |
| 3 | The story of Élisabeth Löwenthal's love letters | ~55% | The prosecutor (Fumeril) believes he has found a motive for murder (the Gautrus stealing the book), but this is a smokescreen laid by Leboc |

##### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

The correct reasoning path in this case is exceptionally difficult. At 38% (the appearance of Vaucherel's portrait), readers who are particularly attentive to "paintings" may notice this realistic amateur work — but at this point there is no way to connect it to the idea that the same painter also produced a canvas for Leboc's window. The 45–50% passage (the visiting card and the boot hobnails) is the only reasoning process Barnett openly displays; careful readers can deduce from it that Leboc is deliberately concealing something, but still cannot confirm he committed the crime. The full solution does not appear until the reveal at 85%.

The fundamental difficulty lies in the fact that "a person appearing in two places at once" is impossible in reality — and for that very reason, the reader's rational mind automatically dismisses the hypothesis that Leboc is the true killer, walking straight into the author's trap.

##### Additional Notes

The "painted alibi" is an extraordinarily rare technique in the history of detective fiction. This story may be one of the earliest formal murder mysteries to use "a static image to falsify a person's presence" (1929). Notably, the trick's premise depends on "the distance of the witnesses" — observers at a remove cannot distinguish a real person from a convincingly realistic oil painting, which makes the deception entirely plausible in visual terms.

At the end of the case, Barnett quietly steals the royal love letters, and three months later sells them back to the Löwenthal family for one hundred thousand francs — along with helping himself to an entire cart of groceries. This ending — justice served on the surface, private profit secured underneath — is the moral humor Leblanc custom-tailored for Lupin.

### Story Three: The Man with the Gold Teeth ("The Man with the Gold Teeth," Chapter Five)

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery types: whodunit + frame-up method (identity confusion)
- Victim: Nine gold and silver religious artifacts from a village church

#### Overall Difficulty Rating

| Indicator | Rating | Description |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Average convergence point approximately 80% — the real culprit (the Baron) is "the helpful neighbor," a counter-intuitive role; the source of the gold leaf is the only traceable clue |
| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ | A systematic frame-up: from footprints and clothing to gold teeth, the entire body of evidence is carefully constructed to point at an innocent merchant |

#### Truth Breakdown

##### Truth One: Who Stole the Church Treasures? (whodunit)
- **Clue convergence point: approximately 80%**
- Answer: Baron de Gravières, the parish priest's neighbor and "good friend," is the real thief. He had spent years observing the traveling merchant Vinçon's annual pilgrimage routine, deliberately mimicking his clothing (gray hat, brown coat), and affixed gold leaf to his teeth to create the mirror-image confusion of "gold teeth on the left side," framing his own crime upon Vinçon's memory.

##### Truth Two: Why Did the Priest "See" the Gold Tooth on the Wrong Side? (howdunit)
- **Clue convergence point: approximately 75%**
- Answer: The priest was startled awake in the middle of the night and, due to sleep inertia, was in fact facing the fireplace (his back to the window) in the dark. When he turned on his flashlight, he did not illuminate the thief directly — he illuminated the thief's reflection in a full-length wardrobe mirror. The mirror reversed left and right, so the "gold tooth on the right" appeared to be on the "left."

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | The full-length portrait in Vaucherel's study (amateur style) and a comparable situation in the church theft case | — | Another key point in this story: a poorly executed painting links two locations |
| 2 | The position of the mirror in the priest's bedroom (the full-length wardrobe mirror facing toward the bed) | ~55% | Barnett points out that in the confusion the priest had his back to the window, facing the fireplace, with the flashlight beam striking the mirror |
| 3 | Barnett places a piece of gold leaf on his own tooth, recreating the "gold tooth" effect | ~78% | Directly reveals the trick: gold leaf can easily simulate the appearance of a gold tooth |
| 4 | A shop in the village had sold a full sheet of gold leaf — the buyer was the Baron | ~80% | **Convergence point**: the only objective piece of evidence directly linking the real culprit to the instrument of the crime |
| 5 | The Baron's house commands a complete view of the church and the priest's residence, and the Baron is fully acquainted with the priest's every anti-theft habit | ~35% | Appears early, but readers tend to overlook its criminal implications after encountering the Baron as "the helpful friend" |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | Vinçon makes regular secret visits every year; his movements are mysterious and he refuses to explain his reasons | ~20% | Makes him a natural suspect (suspicious behavior itself is the bait) |
| 2 | Vinçon's shoe prints match footprints found at the scene | ~30% | The Baron had observed and replicated Vinçon's shoe pattern in advance, or used Vinçon's actual shoes |
| 3 | The Baron has recently been in financial difficulty and has placed his valuable antique furniture in storage | ~70% | Establishes a motive for the crime |

##### Red Herrings

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Misleading Direction |
|---|-------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Vinçon's mysterious annual visits on a fixed date (the true reason: he comes to mourn his deceased lover) | Throughout | Convinces everyone that he is a long-premeditated church thief |
| 2 | Footprints and clothing descriptions found at the scene consistent with Vinçon's characteristics | ~25–30% | All systematic physical evidence points to the innocent party |
| 3 | The priest clearly states the gold tooth was on the left side, while Vinçon's tooth is on the right | ~55% | This should be the key to exposing the frame-up, but it is dismissed by Béchoux's eagerness to extract a confession |

##### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

At 35% (the Baron's house overlooking the church), readers who hold to the principle that "the least suspicious person is the suspect" can theoretically begin to consider the Baron — but this thought is almost impossible to form at that moment, because the Baron is precisely "the person offering help," "the person who led the detectives to the scene."

The 55–60% passage (the contradiction in the gold tooth's position) is the most important puzzle in this case and the core of Barnett's eventual solution. Careful readers can pause here to wonder "if Vinçon is not the thief, who is?" and from there work backwards to the Baron's possible involvement. But both the "gold leaf trick" and the "mirror illusion" can hardly be confirmed until the 75–80% mark.

##### Additional Notes

The double-layered trick in this story — frame-up of identity combined with mirror-image perceptual error — is technically quite rare. The "mirror reversal of left and right" solution in particular exploits the priest's half-awake perceptual confusion, making it a clever "cognitive science puzzle," remarkably ahead of its time in the detective literature of the 1920s.

Vinçon's love story — secretly making a pilgrimage every year to mourn his deceased lover "Angélique" — is the most moving character sketch in the entire collection. The innocent man appears suspicious for precisely the reason that his deepest secret is his most pure emotion. This moral sorrow — "goodness misread as malice" — elevates this story beyond the ordinary puzzle framework.

### Story Four: A Game of Baccarat ("A Game of Baccarat," Chapter Four)

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery types: whodunit + motive reconstruction
- Victim: Gambler Paul Erstein, beaten to death with brass knuckles

#### Overall Difficulty Rating

| Indicator | Rating | Description |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Average convergence point approximately 75% — the main suspects (Dujaune vs. Forgeray) can be identified early, but the ultimate killer Tuillier is sufficiently concealed |
| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | All physical evidence points to the least suspicious person (Forgeray); the silence of three "accomplices" creates collective cover |

#### Truth Breakdown

##### Truth One: Who Killed Erstein? (whodunit)
- **Clue convergence point: approximately 75%**
- Answer: Maxime Tuillier, one of the four players, followed Erstein into the rotunda room after losing all his savings at baccarat, beat him to death with brass knuckles, then tampered with the dead man's watch to falsify the time of death, stole the banknotes Erstein had won, and fabricated the story that "Erstein voluntarily returned the losers' money."

##### Truth Two: Do the Three Factory Owners Constitute Accomplices? (whydunit)
- **Clue convergence point: approximately 85%**
- Answer: The three men did not know the murder had occurred, but they strongly suspected Tuillier had used violent means, and after accepting the returned banknotes they deliberately maintained their silence to protect their reputations and financial interests.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | After winning a large sum, Erstein suddenly "generously returns" the money — extremely unusual behavior for a professional gambler | ~30% | The so-called "return" is pure fabrication, a scenario invented by Tuillier after committing the murder |
| 2 | Tuillier's finances are on the verge of bankruptcy; he cannot repay his debts by month's end | ~55% | Confirms the motive: what he lost is not merely the wager, but the pressure of total financial collapse |
| 3 | The murder weapon (brass knuckles) is buried in a flowerpot on the balcony adjacent to the crime scene — but that balcony belongs to Madame Forgeray | ~35% | The most misleading piece of physical evidence: the weapon appears in the nearest adjacent building |
| 4 | The three factory owners said not a single word during the drive home (as testified by the coachman) | ~60% | Reveals they knew full well what had happened, already psychologically implanted with the fear of speaking |
| 5 | The baccarat reconstruction: Béchoux keeps winning (unlike the original session where Tuillier "lost"), and Tuillier's expression betrays him | ~75% | Convergence point — the scene recreation forces Tuillier to psychologically relive the crime, causing him to break down and confess |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Description |
|---|-------------|------------------------|-------------|
| 1 | The dead man's watch hands have been manually set (showing "four fifty-five," but the actual time of death was earlier) | ~28% | Indicates someone deliberately created a time gap after the murder to support the fiction that Erstein "died after leaving" |
| 2 | The rotunda room opens onto the club balcony, from which one can leap to the neighboring building's balcony (the Forgeray residence) | ~32% | Explains the weapon's location — the killer hid it in the flowerpot after accessing the adjacent balcony |
| 3 | Madame Forgeray had corresponded with Erstein; a letter mentions that her husband "is very jealous and may lose control" | ~40% | Reinforces suspicion of Forgeray, yet is in fact an innocent person's inadvertent self-description |

##### Red Herrings

| # | Clue Content | Appearance Position (%) | Misleading Direction |
|---|-------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| 1 | The murder weapon buried in Forgeray's flowerpot, and Forgeray's absence late at night | ~35–40% | Convinces the police (including Béchoux) that Forgeray is the killer |
| 2 | Madame Forgeray's love letter: "my husband is extremely jealous and may take extreme action" | ~40% | Perfectly completes the "jealous husband kills rival" motive narrative |
| 3 | Forgeray himself says "I am the murderer" (upon first arriving at the scene) | ~25% | He believed his wife had been killed; his panicked self-recrimination is misread as a confession |

##### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

By 30–35% (the weapon found on Forgeray's balcony, Forgeray out late at night, his wife's letter), readers will find it nearly impossible not to fix on Forgeray as the primary suspect — the physical evidence is simply too complete.

For readers who grow suspicious of "evidence that is too complete," the 30% mark offers the anomaly of "Erstein suddenly returning his winnings" and the 60% mark reveals "the collective silence of the three factory owners" as a sign of cover for an accomplice. But to identify Tuillier specifically — rather than any of the three owners — requires the scene reconstruction at 75% for confirmation.

The reasoning challenge of this story lies in the fact that readers must simultaneously "discard a primary suspect backed by overwhelming evidence" and "locate the real killer among five players" — a double-elimination puzzle of high difficulty.

##### Additional Notes

The "baccarat reconstruction" is the most dramatically accomplished technique in this story: Barnett impersonates Tuillier to recreate the psychological state before the murder, using the visual power of the stage to make the true killer collapse and confess on his own. This is more theatrically compelling than the traditional "accusation → rebuttal → physical evidence" model, and reflects Leblanc's distinctive interrogation philosophy: "never attack a suspect head-on; instead, recreate the scene so their psychology disintegrates."

It is worth noting that the three factory owners' "moral complicity" receives no legal punishment, only Barnett's verbal judgment. Leblanc uses this to explore a deeper moral question: does silent acquiescence constitute a crime?

## Overall Series Reasoning Path

The central "mystery within a mystery" of *Arsène Lupin Intervenes* is the identity puzzle that runs through the entire book: who exactly is Jim Barnett?

The very first page of the preface hints that "his methods bring to mind that person who gave the Paris police such headaches," and the final pages explicitly reveal "that is Arsène Lupin!" — but this revelation is completed in the preface itself, making it not a mystery but a meta-framework.

The truly interesting deductive exercise lies in this question: at what point can the reader deduce Barnett's true intentions from "the way Barnett collects his fees"? At the end of each story, he always arranges to "acquire" something in some clever fashion: a pearl necklace, a royal love letter, a piece of antique furniture, an industrial formula… These patterns of "payment" are themselves the clues for identifying the identity of Arsène Lupin.

## Additional Notes

**Leblanc's Late-Period Stylistic Shift**

Compared to the early Lupin stories (such as *Arsène Lupin vs. Holmlock Shears*), *Arsène Lupin Intervenes* reveals an important transformation in Leblanc's approach: Lupin no longer appears as "the hero who robs the rich to help the poor," but instead uses the disguise of "private detective" to insert himself into cases while simultaneously pursuing his own gain. This design gives every story a double ending — justice overtly served, and Lupin quietly pocketing something on the side.

**Variety of Trick Types**

The technical highlight of this collection lies in the diversity of its tricks:
- Chapter Two: hidden location for stolen goods (the drainpipe)
- Chapter Three: false alibi (the painting)
- Chapter Four: collective silence creating misdirection
- Chapter Five: identity frame-up (gold leaf teeth + mirror illusion)
- Chapter Six: an object that is "simultaneously inside and outside" (the minister's briefcase)
- Chapter Seven: a female criminal + accidentally killing the wrong person
- Chapter Eight: an invisible murder method involving cutting a rope
- Chapter Nine: an entry method using "twin" substitution

This variety makes the collection resistant to easy categorization, and ensures each story makes an independent technical contribution.

**Comparison with Contemporaries**

In 1929, Agatha Christie was developing the ideas that would become *Murder on the Orient Express*, and the tradition of Poe had evolved into the rule-bound system of the Golden Age. Leblanc's approach was always "entertainment over fairness" — his puzzles do not necessarily follow the rule that all clues must be planted in advance, but every solution has sufficient narrative logic.

Barnett/Lupin is not a detective; he is an artist who plays the system. And *Arsène Lupin Intervenes* is not strictly a mystery novel — it is a moral satire dressed in the clothing of puzzles, laughing as it asks: can justice and self-interest ever truly coexist?

**Note for Readers**

"Baccarat" in the fourth chapter of this collection is the central prop of the murder case. It is a gambling game that was enormously popular in French high society from the nineteenth to early twentieth centuries, structured around a banker-versus-player wager in which the outcome depends heavily on luck, though there is also room for psychological manipulation. Leblanc's choice of baccarat as the backdrop for a murder precisely captures the psychological arc of "a single night's reversal → emotional collapse → murder."
