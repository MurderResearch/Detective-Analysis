# The Teeth of the Tiger (The Teeth of the Tiger) — Deducibility Analysis

## Basic Information
- Author: Maurice Leblanc
- Year: 1914
- Type: Novel
- Detective: Arsène Lupin / Don Luis Perenna (Don Luis Perenna, alias of Arsène Lupin)
- Core Mystery Type: whodunit + howdunit + multiple truths (mastermind behind the scenes vs. first-layer killer)

## Synopsis

French millionaire Cosmo Mornington is apparently murdered in Paris, leaving behind a vast fortune with instructions that it be inherited by "Arsène Lupin" — a man he has never met in person, only corresponded with by letter — on the condition that Lupin investigate the cause of his death. Lupin appears under the false name Don Luis Perenna and begins his investigation.

As the investigation unfolds, a vast murder network comes to light: detective Vérot dies of poisoning, but before his death leaves behind a sealed report pointing to impending doom for the Fauville family. Shortly thereafter, Hippolyte Fauville and his son Edmond die in succession, and police ultimately arrest Fauville's wife Marie and her lover Raoul Sauverand on murder charges. All evidence points to Marie — she was seen wandering near the scene late at night, her jewellery box contained a spare key, and forensic experts confirmed that a tooth mark found on an apple in the garden matched her exactly.

But Perenna never believes Marie is guilty. During his investigation he survives multiple assassination attempts, mysterious threatening letters keep appearing, and a chain of deaths forms an ever-deeper murder chain. He ultimately uncovers two layers of truth: the first layer reveals that Fauville was in fact the **architect** of the entire murder — this electrical engineer suffering from a terminal illness had, before his death, meticulously devised a "ghost machine" to frame his wife from beyond the grave, while he himself was the one who killed his son and himself. The second layer reveals that Fauville was merely a pawn — the true manipulator behind the scenes was a lame invalid named Jean Vernocq, who had barely been mentioned at all, and who, seeing a loophole in Mornington's will, schemed to seize the enormous inheritance of one hundred million francs by eliminating all other heirs and gaining control over the last remaining heir, Florence (Florence Levasseur).

## Truth Analysis

### Truth One: Fauville's "Ghost Crime" — A Suicidal Perfect Frame-Up (howdunit + first-layer whodunit)

- **Convergence point: approximately 80%**
- Answer: Fauville was suffering from late-stage cancer and his son Edmond had tuberculosis; he knew full well that his end was near. Months before his death he had planned everything: he installed a precision clock mechanism inside the chandelier of his study, allowing pre-written letters to be delivered on schedule after his death, thereby framing his wife and her lover as killers who "murdered him in pursuit of freedom." He personally poisoned his son, then took his own life with the same poison. He placed an apple bearing a forged tooth mark in the garden as physical evidence. He disguised himself in advance as Sauverand, poisoned detective Vérot who had uncovered part of the truth, and swapped out his sealed report. The entire scheme took death as its starting point and the grave as its command post, directing every trace of murder toward his innocent wife.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Fauville says "tomorrow night at the earliest," denying any danger tonight | ~18% | He knows exactly his plan's timetable but plays along pretending ignorance — in hindsight, this is "the earliest lie" |

### Truth Two: The Mastermind Jean Vernocq's Inheritance Murder Chain (whydunit + second-layer whodunit)

- **Convergence point: approximately 92%**
- Answer: Jean Vernocq — a destitute criminal suffering from spinal disease and tuberculosis, severely limited in mobility — upon reading Mornington's will by chance, saw a perfect opportunity: if all the heirs could be eliminated, leaving Florence Levasseur (in reality the illegitimate daughter of the late Raoul Sauverand) as the sole legal heir, and then gaining control through marriage, he could possess one hundred million francs. He first poisoned Mornington, then using his knowledge of Fauville's private life, guided this terminally ill man down the path of self-destructive revenge while covertly controlling the direction of those posthumous letters. He arranged for Marie and Sauverand to commit suicide in prison, made four attempts on Perenna's life, and finally kidnapped Florence in an attempt to silence her — at the end of this murder chain, he alone stood to gain everything.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Mornington's will is designed with a "contingent heir" structure, with Luis himself among the beneficiaries | ~5% | Establishes the "who benefits" motivational framework; simultaneously plants a false trail making Luis a suspect |
| 2 | Sauverand reveals he is the son of Victor Sauverand, whose mother was an old acquaintance of the Mornington family | ~42% | Establishes the inheritance order: Fauville family → Sauverand → Florence; the complete structure of motive becomes clear from this point |
| 3 | The "five dates" memo in volume eight of Shakespeare | ~38% | Someone knew in advance the delivery schedule of Fauville's clock mechanism — namely Vernocq — he is a co-conspirator, not merely a bystander |
| 4 | Langernault had been dead for four years, yet Fauville continued writing letters to him | ~62% | Those letters must have been intercepted and used by someone; Vernocq is that interceptor, holding the secret that kept Fauville believing Langernault was still alive |
| 5 | Two skeletons are found in the barn of an old castle (the remains of the Desoulamart couple) | ~55% | Vernocq's history of killing predates the Paris series of cases — he is a serial killer, not someone acting on impulse |
| 6 | Anonymous threatening letters are traced to a nursing home on Avenue d'Avon | ~88% | Directly narrows down Vernocq's hiding place; the "patient" identity at the nursing home explains why he barely appeared in public |
| 7 | Florence confirms she is the illegitimate daughter of Raoul Sauverand | ~90% | The final piece of the puzzle — confirms Florence is the sole true legal heir; Vernocq's entire motivational structure is complete at this point |
| 8 | The nursing home director admits to having received anonymous instructions; Vernocq is that "patient" | ~92% | Vernocq's identity is finally confirmed; the control chain of behind-the-scenes manipulation is completely exposed |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | The will drawer in the lawyer's office has been forced open | ~12% | Someone outside already knew the contents of Mornington's will before the events began — the origin of the motive |
| 2 | The identity of the "doctor" who issued a false death certificate for Mornington is suspicious | ~8% | Murdering Mornington required a medical accomplice; Vernocq's network had already reached into medical institutions |
| 3 | Vernocq is lame, but witnesses variously describe the suspect as tall or stooped | ~93% | His physical condition makes his appearance elusive; this contradiction is the reason for his long-term concealment and the key reason Perenna identifies him only at the last moment |
| 4 | Florence repeatedly appears near crime scenes but cannot provide reasonable explanations for her movements | ~55% | She is indirectly manipulated by Vernocq, becoming the final pawn in his plan, completely unaware of this |

| 2 | Planning documents related to the case are found hidden in Florence's bookshelf | ~38% | Strongly implies she is a knowing accomplice; in fact planted by Vernocq to make her appear guilty |

### Truth Three: The Tooth Mark Mystery (howdunit — the final puzzle deliberately left unresolved)

- **Convergence point: approximately 95% (the author intentionally withholds part of the answer)**
- Answer: Fauville states explicitly in his confession letter: "That apple was not bitten by Marie, but it bears her tooth mark." He somehow replicated his wife's tooth mark and pressed it into the apple — creating false physical evidence that even forensic experts could not distinguish from genuine. The exact forgery technique is deliberately left unrevealed by Leblanc: Perenna says "when I have physical proof, I will explain to you all," and then never explains. This deliberate gap is at once the most malevolent symbol of Fauville's scheme — he used his wife's physiological characteristics with scientific cold-bloodedness as a weapon to destroy her — and also the author's final slight torture of the reader: the most fundamental "how was it done" you will never know in full.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Forensic experts confirm the tooth mark matches Marie perfectly, "scientifically irrefutable" | ~33% | Sets the standard of the puzzle — the forgery must achieve precision that forensic science cannot detect |
| 2 | Fauville's confession letter explicitly states "it was not bitten by her, but bears her tooth mark" | ~80% | Confirms the forgery exists, but does not explain the method; the puzzle shifts from "was it forged?" to "how was it forged?" |
| 3 | Perenna's promise to "explain when I have physical evidence" | ~82% | The author uses Perenna's voice to tell readers: the method exists, but will not be revealed |

## Reasoning Path Reconstruction

**First Reasoning Path: Fauville is the Killer (The Victim Reversed)**

The earliest signal appears in the first chapter (8%): Fauville is an electrical engineer with expertise in machinery. The average reader will not immediately connect this information to a "ghost machine," but it is the foundation of the entire reasoning path. The first moment that gives a careful reader genuine pause is 24%: the watch stopped at 12:30, and the time of death is earlier than any point when Perenna was present — meaning the "protected person" was already dead before being protected, and the logic of outside intervention does not hold.

Yet the detail that "the door was locked from the inside with no sign of forced entry" (22%) is already sufficient for the most perceptive reader to suspect: this is not murder at all, but suicide. But if it is suicide, why frame the wife? The clock mechanism in the chandelier (78%) becomes the decisive physical evidence here — the only reason for that device to exist is to allow a dead man to continue "acting" after death. At this point, the outline of "Fauville's suicide and scheme to frame his wife" is complete. The confession letter (80%) merely confirms in words what the machine had already told the reader.

**Second Reasoning Path: Vernocq is the Mastermind Behind the Scenes**

This path gives readers almost no early entry point. The most deceptive design in the entire book lies in the fact that it allows you, having discovered the first layer of truth (Fauville), to believe the puzzle has been solved — and it is precisely at that moment that the second layer begins.

The first truly useful clue appears at 55%: the two skeletons in the barn, indicating that this murder chain already existed before the Fauville family events, and that there is a long-term operator completely indifferent to killing. The "Shakespeare memo" at 38% is an earlier but more cryptic signal — someone has knowledge of Fauville's plan schedule, indicating there must be an external accomplice. The "Langernault dead four years" clue at 62% makes this external accomplice clearer: he must have maintained long-term access to Fauville's correspondence to guide him down this path.

Not until the nursing home trace at 88%, Florence's identity confirmed at 90%, and the director's testimony at 92% does Vernocq fully emerge from the fog. The average reader has virtually no chance of deducing his existence before this — his design goal was to be invisible from the start.

## Supplementary Notes

**The Special Place of *The Teeth of the Tiger* in the History of Detective Fiction**

This novel is one of Leblanc's most precise and most ruthless murder structures. Its distinctiveness lies in the fact that it is simultaneously a detective novel in which "the victim is the killer," and a meta-puzzle in which "the detective may also have been set up as a suspect."

The Lupin series has long been centred on the image of the "gentleman thief," and readers' sympathy for him makes the design of "he is also a suspect" particularly effective: whenever police or readers suspect him, that suspicion carries a sense of relaxation — a feeling that "the author is teasing us." But this time Leblanc does not entirely dissolve that suspicion; instead he allows it to overlap perfectly with the true killer Vernocq's motive, and only disassembles it at the very end.

**The Narrative Design of the Three-Layer Onion Structure**

The true innovation of this book is the "three-layer onion":
- **First layer (surface)**: Marie and Sauverand are the killers (the police version)
- **Second layer (reversal)**: Fauville is the architect behind the scenes (suicide as frame-up)
- **Third layer (deep reversal)**: Vernocq is the true mastermind (Fauville himself is merely his pawn)

The problem with this three-layer structure is that it is extremely unfair to the reader: the clues for the third layer are almost entirely invisible within the fog of the first and second layers. Vernocq's name barely appears in the first 80% of the story — he is an answer forcibly inserted from outside the text, rather than a conclusion the reader can deduce from existing clues. Leblanc here chose the narrative impact of shock, at the expense of the fairness of the puzzle.

**Comparison with Christie**

Both employ a "multiple truths" structure, but Agatha Christie in similar designs (such as *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd*, 1926) still adheres to the basic rule of "all clues publicly visible." Leblanc is clearly not bound by this rule: his puzzle design serves the story's sense of surprise and rhythm, not the reader's fair-play experience of solving the mystery. This is not a flaw — it is two entirely different aesthetics of detective fiction. The Lupin series never promised to be "fair play"; it is a hybrid of puzzle and adventure, and *The Teeth of the Tiger* is the most refined, and most ruthless, specimen of that hybrid.

**The Literary Significance of the Tooth Mark Puzzle**

The deliberately incomplete revelation at the end of the book — "how was the tooth mark replicated" — is a literary choice worth reflecting on. It ensures that Fauville's malice retains a layer of shadow even after it is exposed: we know he did it, but we do not know how. This incompleteness makes him a more unsettling criminal: his technical ability exceeds the range that the detective (and the reader) can fully comprehend, and that feeling of "unable to fully understand" is precisely the final impression Leblanc wants to leave in the reader's mind.

**A Note for Readers**

The one hundred million francs in Mornington's will, converted at 1914 purchasing power, would amount to approximately several billion in today's currency — this explains why this sum was worth one man's years of effort and multiple lives in pursuit. The Paris settings in the book — from Rue de la Comtesse to the nursing home on Avenue d'Avon — are the real urban spaces Leblanc habitually employed, providing his puzzles with solid geographical credibility.
