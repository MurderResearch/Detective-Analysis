# The Eight Strokes of the Clock — Deducibility Analysis

## Basic Information
- Author: Maurice Leblanc
- Year: 1923
- Type: Short story collection (eight stories total)
- Detective: Prince Renine — i.e., Arsène Lupin appearing under an assumed name
- Core mystery types: whodunit / howdunit (varies by story)

---

## Overall Deducibility Rating

| Metric | Rating | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Average convergence point approximately 75% — the key clues for each story's solution tend to arrive only in the second half, and require a considerable degree of logical leaping |
| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐ | Moderate misdirection: Leblanc's fog does not rely on false clues but rather on "narrative selectivity" — the detective knows far more than the reader, and the truly crucial information is typically withheld until the final reveal |

> **On the special nature of this collection:** Renine (Lupin) is an actor, not an observer. He makes extensive use of psychological pressure, trickery, and improvisation; in many stories the "solution" is not built up gradually from clues in the text but is instead forced out by his overwhelming willpower. This places the collection closer to "crime thriller" than pure detective fiction. That said, in several stories Leblanc does embed clues that an attentive reader could use to solve the mystery independently. The four stories with the highest deductive content are analyzed in depth below.

---

## Synopsis

The collection is structured around "eight strokes of the clock": an old clock in an abandoned castle suddenly strikes eight, and the detective Renine (i.e., Lupin) uses this as the basis for a wager with the heroine Hortense Daniel — eight adventures, each resolving a different mystery, culminating in the recovery of a stolen amulet as the final condition. If all conditions are met, Hortense must grant him "an appropriate reward."

The eight stories revolve around murder, serial killing, faked death, kidnapping, and the concealment of stolen goods. The settings range across the Norman countryside, the streets of Paris, Breton manor houses, and the banks of the Seine. In each story Renine resolves the case through forceful logic and sheer force of will (occasionally augmented by trickery), while gradually developing a romantic connection with Hortense. The collection ends with the clock striking for the eighth time and the two of them united at last.

### Story Two: The Water-Bottle

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery types: whodunit + howdunit (cause of fire)
- Background: Jacques Aubrieux has been sentenced to death for murdering a cousin and stealing sixty thousand francs; he is to be executed the following morning.

---

#### Truth One: Who Is the Real Killer? (whodunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 38%**
- Answer: His childhood friend Gaston Dutreuil. He is the only person who simultaneously knew two things: that the cousin had just received sixty thousand francs, and that Jacques had decided not to go out riding that day. He "disappeared" for two hours during a cinema visit, rode Jacques's motorcycle to Suresnes to commit the murder, then calmly reappeared at the cinema exit afterward.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Mme Aubrieux told Dutreuil by telephone: the cousin had just received sixty thousand francs, and Jacques had decided not to go out that day | ~20% | The only person who simultaneously satisfied both conditions was Dutreuil — this is the single most important clue in the story, but it is buried so casually that the reader must actively recognize the "uniqueness" of the situation |
| 2 | During Dutreuil's time at the cinema, the two ladies accompanying him never saw him for the entire performance | ~40% | A two-hour window — more than enough time to ride to Suresnes and back; this is the direct evidence from which his absence can be inferred |
| 3 | Dutreuil's top-floor garret apartment "happened" to catch fire on the very day of the murder | ~68% | The timing is far too convenient; the fire almost certainly confirms he was hiding banknotes there |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | After reading the newspaper in the restaurant, Dutreuil's face went pale and he left hastily | ~8% | An emotional reaction that exceeds mere concern for a friend, suggesting personal stakes |
| 2 | Dutreuil's apartment is immaculately tidy, with every object in its assigned place | ~43% | A personality trait: he craves a sense of control — such a person would not passively watch his cover collapse |
| 3 | When Dutreuil received news of the fire, the corner of his mouth flickered with a faint smile | ~69% | A person who smiles at a fire is the arsonist; this is the moment of key confirmation |

##### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------------|
| 1 | At the scene: Jacques's motorcycle tire tracks, his handkerchief, his revolver | Background information | Leads the court (and the reader) to conclude Jacques is the killer |
| 2 | Dutreuil displays deep friendship and grief on behalf of his friend Jacques | ~10–30% | Causes the reader to rule out the possibility that he is the killer |

---

#### Truth Two: How Did the Fire Start with No One to Light It? (howdunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 90%**
- Answer: Dutreuil soaked the hat box, the banknotes, and thin paper in flammable liquid, then — before leaving — placed a round-bellied crystal water bottle on the window ledge facing the morning sun, using its focusing effect to ignite the materials: the "magnifying lens" principle, requiring no one to be present.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Dutreuil opened the water bottle beside the window, took a few sips, and then left it on the window ledge | ~56% | The author describes this action deliberately but without emphasis — at this moment the reader is almost certain to miss its significance |
| 2 | Before the search of the apartment, Dutreuil stood facing the window with his back to the others, basking in the sunlight for quite some time | ~55% | Suggests he is calculating the angle and timing of the sunlight |
| 3 | Once the fire was extinguished, only a scorched hat box and thin paper remained — no ignition device of any kind | ~72% | The only coherent explanation is "remote ignition," and the room happens to contain a round-bellied water bottle |

##### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------------|
| 1 | Dutreuil left in full view of everyone — he "could not possibly" have started the fire | ~65% | Leads everyone present (including the police) to rule out his being the arsonist |

---

#### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

**Whodunit path:**

By approximately 20% of the story, the reader ought to feel a stirring of suspicion: Mme Aubrieux's telephone call simultaneously conveyed two crucial pieces of information (the sixty thousand francs; Jacques not going out) to three people, and Dutreuil was one of them. By 40%, the "two-hour disappearance at the cinema" allows the logic to close: he is the only person with the opportunity, the motive, and the means (the motorcycle sitting in the garage). Renine makes his conclusion explicit before 70%, but an attentive reader could have arrived there independently before 40%.

**Howdunit (water bottle) path:**

This line of reasoning cannot be independently worked out by the reader — the detail of the water bottle on the window ledge drifts in and out like ordinary scene-setting. This is Leblanc's deliberate "fair hiding": the clue is there, but before the reveal it is almost impossible to connect it to the principle of the focusing lens. This puzzle is more of an "ah, of course!" after the fact than an "I should have seen that coming."

---

#### Supplementary Notes

The finest quality of this story lies in its "reasoning under a moral dilemma": Renine has no actual evidence, only intuition and logical inference, yet he must extract the truth before the execution the following morning. He bluffs his way in (claiming to know where the banknotes are hidden), draws the suspect out, substitutes a fake hat box to manufacture a scene, and finally — by pretending that the suspect has already given himself away — forces a written confession. This is stratagems, not evidence. Leblanc implicitly asks: when the truth is clear but proof is absent, is deception justified?

### Story Five: Thérèse and Germaine

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery types: howdunit (locked-room crime) + whydunit
- Surface puzzle: Jacques d'Ormeval walked in full view of witnesses into a sealed beach hut, locked the door, and was shortly afterward discovered dead of a stab wound to the back — no one entered, no one left, the weapon vanished. Who is the killer?

---

#### Truth One: The Locked-Room Mystery — How Was d'Ormeval Killed Inside a Sealed Hut? (howdunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 72%**
- Answer: There was no locked room at all. D'Ormeval had already been stabbed before he entered the hut — while he was bent over on the steps below the hut searching for the key, his wife Thérèse stabbed him in the back with a dagger from behind. He then stood up, acted as if nothing had happened, walked to the hut, and quietly died inside. The physical basis of this mystery is that a person can continue to move for a short time after being stabbed.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | The husband and wife both bent down at the foot of the steps to search for the key, creating a brief blind spot | ~30% | If the reader asks "could anything have happened during this interval?" the answer is yes — this is the window of opportunity for the murder |
| 2 | D'Ormeval afterward walked normally to the hut and even waved to the card players | ~32% | This detail is meant to reinforce "no one could walk after being killed" — but in fact a knife wound need not be immediately fatal |
| 3 | The hut had no windows; it was completely impossible to see inside from outside | ~40% | Reinforces the "locked room" illusion, but by the same token confirms that no outsider could have committed the crime after he entered |
| 4 | Renine's counter-question: "If no one went in, the murder must have occurred before he went in" | ~75% | This is the logical backbone of the story; Renine solves the locked-room mystery by pure elimination |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Afterward, Thérèse stood on the terrace "calmly" watching, and only collapsed when news arrived | ~35% | This abnormal composure, contrasted with her eventual hysteria, is actually guilt and inner struggle |
| 2 | Germaine says "I guessed at once," implying she had already suspected someone | ~60% | Germaine knows more than the reader; her reaction serves as a pointer to the clues |

##### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------------|
| 1 | The entire puzzle is framed as a "locked-room murder" | First 70% of story | Leads the reader to look inside the hut for secret mechanisms or hidden passages, rather than thinking outside the hut |
| 2 | The Astaing siblings are presented in the first half of the story as conspirators plotting to kill Thérèse | ~20% | Makes the reader suspect they arranged the killing, rather than that Thérèse acted in her own interest |

---

#### Truth Two: Where Did the Weapon Come From, and Who Is the Killer? (whodunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 82%**
- Answer: The wife Thérèse struck the blow. That morning she had accidentally found a photograph that had slipped from her husband's pocket book — her own face had been replaced by Germaine's, with their two daughters nestled at the rival's side. This triggered her breakdown; gripping a dagger she had already been carrying (originally for self-defense), she acted on impulse.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Germaine says: "The dagger is in your bag — it has your husband's blood on it" | ~82% | This is a direct and explicit accusation, and the reader's first indication of who possessed the weapon |
| 2 | After the Astaing siblings arrived at the scene, the brother Frédéric took the opportunity to feel the outside of Thérèse's bag | ~58% | In hindsight: he was checking for the dagger, not stealing anything — which means they already knew the truth |
| 3 | Thérèse confesses: she saw a photograph in her husband's pocket in which her own face had been replaced by Germaine's | ~88% | The motive for the crime is finally revealed; the reader at last has a complete psychological portrait |

##### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------------|
| 1 | From overheard conversation on the train, Renine learned "someone is plotting to harm someone" | ~5% | The reader assumes the intended victim is Thérèse and the plotters are the siblings — in reality it is exactly the reverse |
| 2 | Thérèse's own image of grief | ~40–65% | Her sorrow is too genuine; the reader is inclined to believe she is the aggrieved party |

---

#### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

**Howdunit path:**

This story is a logical puzzle that requires only one proposition: "No one entered the hut" → "The murder occurred before he entered." By 32% the reader possesses all the necessary premises (no one came or went after entry; a brief blind spot), but almost no reader will think in this direction at that moment, because the "locked room" frame has been firmly established. If around 40% — upon reading "the hut was completely sealed, nothing visible from outside" — the reader actively asks "then how did it happen... wait, could he already have been injured when he went in?" — the mystery can be solved independently before 72%.

**Whodunit path:**

Who had the opportunity, and who had the dagger, are the keys. Thérèse is the only person who was in the blind spot with her husband (30%), and Germaine's reaction — "I knew immediately it was her" (60%) — amounts to pointing out the answer directly. Confirmation must wait until the dagger accusation at 82%.

---

#### Supplementary Notes

This is the most tightly structured story in *The Eight Strokes of the Clock*. Leblanc uses the "locked room" shell to encase a domestic tragedy born of love turned to hatred, and places at its moral center "the victim protecting the perpetrator" (d'Ormeval, dying, refused to accuse his wife), raising a question deeper than the solution itself: when a person chooses silence, does justice still apply? Renine ultimately chooses to let Thérèse go free — because of the two children. This is Lupin-style "outlaw justice": neither law nor morality, but his own personal emotional judgment.

### Story Six: The Lady with the Hatchet

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery types: whodunit (identity of a serial killer) + whydunit (motive)
- Background: Over eighteen months, six women have disappeared in the suburbs of Paris, each found dead a week later with a vertical axe-wound precisely in the center of the forehead, hands and feet bound, carriage wheel tracks nearby. A dropped notebook reveals the name of the seventh intended victim — Hortense, the heroine herself.

---

#### Truth One: Who Is the Killer? (whodunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 85%**
- Answer: Hermance, the former wife of M. de Lourtier-Vaneau — a madwoman secretly installed in a private psychiatric facility at Ville d'Avray. Her husband, living with another woman, had placed her there under the guise of a pension arrangement twenty years earlier and had never given her another thought.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | The names of the six victims: Honorine, Hermione, Hilairie, Hortense… all begin with H and all contain exactly eight letters | ~30% | The letter pattern is the single most important clue in the story, and the data is entirely in plain sight. A reader alert to the names could notice this before Renine does |
| 2 | The murder weapon is a "hatchet" — also beginning with H, matching the initial letter of the victims' names | ~30% | Further confirmation: the killer has an obsession with the letter H / the number 8 |
| 3 | When de Lourtier heard the H-name pattern, his face changed dramatically and cold sweat broke out | ~78% | He recognized something — at this moment the reader ought to realize he is connected to the killer |
| 4 | De Lourtier revealed that his former wife's name is Hermance — a mentally disturbed woman | ~88% | The full truth emerges: Hermance = H + 8 letters, and she is a woman who has been isolated away |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | The technical execution of all the murders is completely identical: the same rope, the same location of the axe wound, a precise interval between killings | ~15% | Mechanical repetition is a characteristic of the "obsessive madwoman" type, suggesting the killer is a monomaniac rather than a rational criminal |
| 2 | De Lourtier's old nurse Felicienne telephoned to find a maid whose name began with H — for "Hermance" | ~72% | This reveals that the psychiatric facility's environment allowed Hermance to make contact with the outside world and select her victims |

##### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------------|
| 1 | The killer has a meticulous plan (the notebook, fixed intervals, pre-arranged disposal sites) — it appears to be rational crime | First 60% of story | Leads the reader (and the police) to assume the killer is an organized criminal, not a pathologically insane person |

---

#### Truth Two: The Killer's Motive — Why Kill? (whydunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 90%**
- Answer: Hermance's two children were killed before her eyes by a carriage when they were young. After this she developed chronic insomnia — for twenty-four hours a day the image of her children's death played before her, and she could not sleep. She accidentally discovered that strangling animals allowed her to fall asleep; from this her obsession developed into: kill a woman whose name resembles her own (beginning with H, eight letters), "steal the woman's sleep," and thereby gain a few months of brief rest — roughly a hundred days at a time.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Hermance could sleep after strangling animals — an observation de Lourtier made several years earlier | ~90% | Reveals the insane logic of "killing = stealing sleep," which is the core of her motive |
| 2 | The numbers in the notebook (132, 118, 128…) represent the number of sleep-days each victim provided | ~90% | Different victims yielded different lengths of sleep, showing that Hermance has her own mad system of calculation |

---

#### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

**Whodunit path:**

The most brilliant element of this story is the "name pattern" clue. When the list of victims appears at 30%, a reader who notices that Honorine (8 letters), Hermione (8 letters), Hortense (8 letters), Hilairie (8 letters) — combined with the murder weapon being a hatchet (H) — can almost independently infer "the killer has an H/8 obsession." But to move from the obsession to "a madwoman," then to "a madwoman hidden in a psychiatric facility," requires following Renine's logic all the way to 88%.

**Whydunit path:**

The motive is almost impossible for the reader to anticipate. "Killing to steal sleep" as an insane premise has no supporting textual clues; it is the product of a madwoman's logic and can only be told to the reader, not deduced. This gives the whydunit in this story a convergence point of 90% — the hardest single question in the entire collection.

---

#### Supplementary Notes

This story occupies a distinctive place in the history of detective fiction: six murders, an entirely identical method, no conventional motive whatsoever — every framework of "rational motive" reasoning is rendered useless. Renine's breakthrough is not tracking clues but first establishing "the killer must be insane," then working backward from the logic of insanity to deduce the standard for victim selection. This method of "using madness to solve the crime" was a remarkably avant-garde conception in 1923.

Even more noteworthy: Hortense herself is chosen as a victim because her name satisfies Hermance's criteria — turning an abstract naming pattern instantly into a direct, personal mortal threat. This is the story's most powerful narrative pivot.

### Story Seven: Footprints in the Snow

#### Basic Information
- Core mystery type: howdunit (physically impossible mystery)
- Background: After a night of snowfall on the estate of Mathias de Gorne, three gunshots are heard; the following morning only the husband's footprints appear in the yard — approaching, but never leaving. Near the well there are signs of a struggle, and nearby is found the revolver of Jérôme Vignal (three rounds discharged). Mathias has vanished from the face of the earth; Jérôme and Mathias's wife Natalie are the prime suspects.

---

#### Truth One: Where Did Mathias Go? (howdunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 78%**
- Answer: Mathias did not die. After Jérôme left, he conceived a scheme — to fabricate his own "murder scene" in order to frame his rival. He walked backward along the very footprints he had made on the way in, placing each foot precisely into an old impression, so that both his outgoing and returning path showed only "inward-facing tracks." He then hid in his father's carriage and escaped to the railway station. The three gunshots were fired by himself, to simulate the sounds of a struggle.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Natalie insists her husband was completely sober when he came home, but the old father's account and the footprints (meandering and unsteady) both say he was drunk | ~60% | This contradiction is the key to the whole story: if he was not drunk, why do the footprints stagger? Answer: they were deliberately faked |
| 2 | The footprints in the snow show Mathias "arriving but never leaving," yet he cannot be found anywhere inside the house | ~20% | "Arriving without leaving" is a physical impossibility — but snow does not lie — so there must be another explanation |
| 3 | Some of the footprints in the snow show an abnormal pattern: heels pressed too deep, toes turned awkwardly, unnatural rhythm | ~83% | After examining the footprints, Renine points out that they are consistent with the characteristics of walking backward, not with a drunken gait |

##### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Old father de Gorne drove off to market that morning as usual, his carriage canvas drawn tight, deliberately evading the investigation | ~25% | His "calm" is incongruous with his son's "disappearance"; something may be concealed under the canvas |
| 2 | The de Gorne father and son had already taken out life insurance policies naming each other as beneficiary | ~85% | If the father "receives the death benefit," he can pass it on to the son — this is the financial motive for faking death |
| 3 | Jérôme claims to have tied Mathias up inside the house and then left — without taking his revolver | ~52% | If Jérôme is telling the truth, the revolver was picked up by Mathias himself to manufacture the scene |

##### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------------|
| 1 | Signs of struggle near the well + revolver with three rounds discharged | ~18% | Strongly implies there is a body in the well and that Jérôme is the killer |
| 2 | The staggering footprints are explained by the old father as "he was drunk" | ~26% | Causes the reader to accept the "drunken gait" explanation and not question the anomaly in the footprints |

---

#### Truth Two: Is Jérôme Guilty? (whodunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 72%**
- Answer: Jérôme is innocent. What he described is entirely true — he went to negotiate with Mathias, offering to purchase the estate in exchange for a divorce agreement; when negotiations broke down the two men fought, he tied Mathias up and left, then carried the unconscious Natalie away with him. His account is entirely consistent, and the very "tidiness" of all the evidence pointing at him is itself a warning sign.

##### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position in story (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-----------------------|-------|
| 1 | Jérôme's testimony and Natalie's testimony match perfectly — and they were questioned separately | ~62% | True conspirators' accounts almost always contain contradictions; perfect agreement instead suggests things happened as they described |
| 2 | Renine says: "When all the evidence points at the same person, it usually means someone arranged it that way" | ~68% | This is the central reasoning principle of the entire story |

---

#### Reasoning Path Reconstruction

The elegance of this story's solution lies in a single "blind spot of common sense": we habitually assume that footprints point in only one direction — forward. But walking backward leaves behind the same "inward-facing tracks."

At what point should the reader notice?

- 20%: The "arriving but never leaving footprints" is already a contradiction, but readers typically lean toward believing "there is a body in the well" rather than questioning the directionality of the footprints.
- 60%: Natalie says her husband was not drunk, but the footprints are unsteady — at this moment, if the reader actively asks "then why are the footprints unsteady?", the answer "walking backward to simulate a drunken gait" becomes almost self-evident.
- 78%: Renine explains the backward-walking footprint logic in front of everyone, and the full picture is revealed.

The earliest possible point at which a reader could solve this is 60% (when the contradiction surfaces), but it requires the reader to actively question the "directionality" assumption about the footprints at that moment.

---

#### Supplementary Notes

"Footprints in the Snow" is the story in this collection that comes closest to classical detective fiction, and its solution rests entirely on one "open and aboveboard" physical fact: a person can walk backward. When Leblanc reveals the answer he writes: "I do not know why we did not think of that sooner." That sentence captures the shared spirit of every successful impossible-crime puzzle — once the solution is revealed, it is obvious; before the revelation, almost no one thinks of it.

This solution was later borrowed and varied by many detective writers, becoming one of the canonical paradigms of the "footprint puzzle."

## Supplementary Notes

**The Place of *The Eight Strokes of the Clock* in Leblanc's Work**

This is one of the most mature short story collections from Leblanc's later period, and one of the few works in which Lupin (under the name "Renine") takes on the role of detective in a straightforwardly positive way. Unlike the early Arsène Lupin series, which sold itself on the premise of "thief-cum-detective," the Renine of this collection more closely resembles a powerful amateur investigator: he does not steal; instead he uses strategy and willpower to help those in distress and unmask criminals, enjoying the thrill of the intellectual contest.

**The Problem of Leblanc's "Unfair Clues"**

One persistent controversy runs through the entire collection: before taking action, Renine typically possesses far more information than the reader (he has conducted his own investigations and arranged preconditions in advance), but these "prior preparations" are disclosed only at the end of each story. Take "The Lady with the Hatchet" as an example: Renine spent four days shut away thinking in solitude and independently worked out the approach through the personal advertisements, but the reader only sees him suddenly spring into action. This means that Leblanc's fog does not rely on false clues but on "the narrator's selective omission of Renine's thought processes" — the reader cannot keep up with the detective not because of misdirection, but because of exclusion.

This technique is a matter of considerable controversy in the history of detective fiction; critics from after the Golden Age (such as Knox's "Ten Commandments") tend to classify it as an "unfair rule." But looked at from the other side, this is also Leblanc's signature quality: his detective is a hero, and heroes do not explain themselves — heroes only present results. The reader's satisfaction comes from the after-the-fact "ah, so that's it!" rather than the competitive pleasure of reasoning in step with the detective.

**Comparison with Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes**

| | Holmes short stories | Renine short stories (*The Eight Strokes of the Clock*) |
|--|--|--|
| Detective's manner | Analytical, composed | Driven by willpower, forceful intervention |
| Reader participation | Relatively high (observing alongside) | Lower (following behind) |
| Source of mystery | Brought by a visitor seeking help | Actively intercepted by the detective |
| Method of solution | Logical deduction | Logic + psychological warfare + trickery |
| Outcome of justice | Usually handed over to the law | Detective adjudicates personally, often bypassing the law |

This difference reflects two cultures' divergent conceptions of the "agent of justice": Holmes is an assistant to the legal order; Lupin/Renine is a substitute for it.
