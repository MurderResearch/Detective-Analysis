# *Arsène Lupin versus Herlock Sholmes* — Detective Analysis

## Basic Information
- Author: Maurice Leblanc
- Year: 1908
- Type: Novella collection (two-part, eight chapters total)
- Detective: Herlock Sholmes, Inspector Ganimard; Arsène Lupin serves simultaneously as criminal and the central axis of deduction
- Core mystery type: whodunit + howdunit + whydunit (dual narrative arcs interwoven)

---

## Ratings Summary

This book is divided into two independent narrative arcs: **"The Blonde Lady"** (Chapters One through Six, approximately 80% of the book) and **"The Jewish Lamp"** (Chapters Seven and Eight, approximately 20%). The two arcs differ sharply in style: the former is a grand chase across Paris, while the latter is a tightly constructed indoor puzzle that delivers a moral reversal in its final moments to shock the reader.

| Metric | Rating | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | "The Blonde Lady" average convergence point approximately 65%; "The Jewish Lamp" average convergence point approximately 91%; overall weighted average approximately 71% |

## Synopsis

### "The Blonde Lady" (Chapters One through Six)

Gerbois, a mathematics professor from Versailles, purchases a small writing desk at a secondhand shop and unwittingly becomes entangled in a wealth-seizure scheme meticulously engineered by Arsène Lupin — for inside the desk's secret drawer lies a lottery ticket worth one million francs. Lupin steals the desk and takes possession of the ticket, but finds himself in a bind: the ticket is in his hands, yet the legal claim belongs to Gerbois. The professor, meanwhile, is coerced into a compromise after his daughter Suzanne is "escorted away" by Lupin as a hostage, and ultimately receives half the prize money.

As this farce draws to a close, a mysterious "Blonde Lady" makes her entrance — first appearing in the bedroom of the murdered Baron d'Hautrec under the name "Antoinette Bréhat," then lying low in the company of the Countess de Crozon under the identity "Madame de Réal," orchestrating the theft of the blue diamond. Inspector Ganimard follows the trail but repeatedly passes within arm's reach of the "true Blonde Lady," only to arrest an innocent diamond broker who happens to share the same name.

While the Paris police stand helpless, the renowned English detective Herlock Sholmes is invited to France, and a ten-day battle of wits with Lupin begins. Sholmes discovers that three crime scenes — the apartment of lawyer Detinan (25 rue Clapeyron), the former residence of Baron d'Hautrec (134 avenue Henri-Martin), and the Château de Crozon — were all designed and built by the same architect, Lucien Destange, and that secret passages connecting adjacent buildings account for everything. Operating under a false name, Sholmes infiltrates Destange's library and finally identifies the true face of the Blonde Lady: **Clotilde Destange**, Destange's daughter and Lupin's longtime secret lover and accomplice.

When Sholmes uses Clotilde as a hostage, Lupin — disguised as a pre-booked taxi driver — calmly abducts both of them and ships Sholmes forcibly onto a vessel bound for England. Sholmes immediately tricks the captain into advancing his watch, disembarks early at Southampton, races back to Paris overnight, and, using an apartment address Lupin inadvertently revealed over the telephone, joins forces with Ganimard to mount a siege. Lupin is ultimately arrested, the blue diamond is retrieved (concealed in the hollow core of Lupin's walking stick), but Clotilde's identity is kept secret by Sholmes alone, in exchange for her freedom.

### "The Jewish Lamp" (Chapters Seven and Eight)

Shortly after returning to Paris, Sholmes receives an invitation from Baron d'Imblevalle to investigate a peculiar theft: someone has broken into the baron's study from outside (ladder, cut window pane) and made off with an ancient Jewish lamp, whose hollow base concealed a gold chimera brooch set with rubies. The police investigation yields nothing, but Lupin sends a letter warning Sholmes not to get involved.

No sooner does Sholmes arrive in Paris than a mysterious young woman intercepts him, imploring him to abandon the investigation and return to England. Sholmes is unmoved. Forty minutes later, he sees through the fiction of "external forced entry": the ladder marks were made by a wooden block, the scratch marks on the balcony were made with sandpaper — the theft came from inside.

The clue is provided unwittingly by little Henriette: the governess Alice Demun habitually cuts letters from newspapers and pastes them into messages. Sholmes discovers the letters cut from a children's primer and reconstructs the corresponding code, tracing a secret channel of communication between Alice and the "Echo de France" personal advertisements column — her line of contact with Lupin. A second clue is a dropped taxi number, linking Alice to a second mysterious address.

A man suspected of being a blackmailer (going by the alias Bresson) kills himself as Sholmes closes in, and the case grows murkier. On the second night, another theft occurs — this time a diamond snuffbox and an opal necklace are also taken, and Wilson is wounded in the process.

In the end, Sholmes encounters Lupin again by the riverbank, disguised as a fishing old man, and the two play out a farcical yet brilliant final confrontation in a leaking boat, which sinks and sends both men swimming back to shore. Sholmes then reveals the full truth: it was Alice who stole the Jewish lamp, but her motive was to protect her mistress. The true injured party is the Baroness Suzanne d'Imblevalle — she had carelessly struck up an acquaintance with Bresson in society, and he had been blackmailing her with her private letters. Alice, to protect her, arranged the theft with Lupin's assistance to satisfy the blackmailer's demands; the second theft, which Bresson engineered independently, was his own act of greed.

The baron learns the truth and the marriage is damaged; Alice departs for England with Sholmes's assistance, while Lupin, already hidden on the same cross-channel steamer, serenely strikes a match.

### Truth One: The Secret Passages Connecting the Three Crime Scenes (howdunit)

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 53% (first half of Chapter Four)**
- Answer: The lawyer Detinan's apartment (25 rue Clapeyron), the former residence of Baron d'Hautrec (134 avenue Henri-Martin), and the Château de Crozon were all designed and built by the same architect, Lucien Destange. Lupin, operating under the alias "Maxime Bermond," was hired to undertake renovation work, and secretly installed secret passages between adjacent buildings — behind bookshelves, within fireplace cavities, through cellars — forming a complete escape network. This structure allowed the Blonde Lady to enter target locations, complete her objectives, and slip away silently through exits in neighboring buildings, leaving behind a mystery with no apparent means of entry or exit.

## Reasoning Path Reconstruction

### "The Blonde Lady": A Reasoning Path from Architecture to Love

Reading Chapter One, the reader witnesses a lively comedy of property seizure, with no element of "mystery" visible — Lupin's identity, methods, and objectives are all transparent. The true mystery surfaces in Chapter Two: **who is the Blonde Lady? How does she appear in and disappear from sealed rooms?**

A careful reader, at Chapter Two (approximately 33%), who notices Ganimard's deduction — "Antoinette Bréhat's hair exactly matches the Blonde Lady's, and she appeared as 'Madame de Réal' the very day after the baron's murder" — should be able to deduce that these are the same person. But her specific identity remains a mystery.

**First key node (approximately 42%)**: Sholmes reads the inscription "Destange, architecte, 1875" on a street corner. This is a small detail, easily overlooked, but for a reader with knowledge of architectural history, it is a nuclear detonation — three "impossible disappearances" suddenly acquire a single, unified physical explanation.

**Second key node (approximately 53%)**: All three crime scenes (rue Clapeyron, avenue Henri-Martin, Château de Crozon) are confirmed to have been designed and built by the same Destange. The reader can now **fully deduce the "how"**: the same architect, secret passages, connecting adjacent buildings, freedom of entry and exit.

**Third key node (approximately 62%)**: Sholmes observes the private interaction between Clotilde and Lupin (operating as Maxime Bermond), realizing she is not merely a passive architect's daughter but an active accomplice. One further step remains: is she the Blonde Lady?

**Convergence point (approximately 75%)**: Sholmes follows the veiled woman through the secret passage and lifts her veil — **Clotilde Destange**, the true face of the Blonde Lady officially revealed. All the mysteries converge here: she used her father's architectural blueprints to move through Paris; her love made her willing to become Lupin's shadow.

### "The Jewish Lamp": A Reasoning Path from a Child's Words to Moral Truth

"The Jewish Lamp" is a "reverse" mystery: the surface crime is quickly seen through (15%), but the true questions — **who? why?** — lie buried until the very end.

**First key node (approximately 15%)**: Sholmes dismantles the fake crime scene and confirms the thief is an insider. But who among those in the house? The baron, the baroness, the servants, the governess, the children.

**Second key node (approximately 45%)**: Henriette's single remark — "the governess cuts out letters and pastes them into telegrams" — sounds like innocent childish chatter, but is in fact the key to the entire communications system. A careful reader, combining this with the clue of "who was it that first tried to stop Sholmes from investigating" (the mysterious young woman at the train station), should by now be able to reasonably suspect that Alice is not merely a nurse but someone implicated in the case.

**Third key node (approximately 50%)**: The cut-out letters reconstruct "REPOND—ECHO—237." This is Alice's communications cipher, pointing to the scheme between her and Lupin.

**Fourth key node (approximately 85%)**: Seven newspaper advertisements rebuild the entire history of the correspondence. Alice is the active supplicant ("540 Lady implores protection"), and the person she is appealing to is Lupin — she provided him with the address and confirmed the date of action.

**Fifth key node (approximately 91%)**: The baron identifies the lock contradiction. Alice's confession collapses logically — she claims to have entered through the study door, but that door was locked from the inside, and on the night in question only the baron and baroness were inside the house.

**Truth convergence (approximately 93%)**: The baroness's breakdown completes the final step. Only now does the reader understand that Alice is a protector, not a culprit; it is the baroness who possessed the "motive" — her past, her momentary credulity regarding Bresson, are the origin of everything. This is the most shattering moral reversal in the entire book: **you believed you had found the "criminal," but she is in fact the most innocent person of all.**

## Supplementary Notes

**Contrasting the Deductive Design of the Two Stories**

The most compelling aspect of this book lies precisely in the fact that the deductive design strategies of the two narrative arcs are diametrically opposed:

| Dimension | "The Blonde Lady" | "The Jewish Lamp" |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Direction of solution | Bottom-up (tracing events back to persons) | Top-down (tracing persons back to motives) |
| Speed of convergence | Gradual, multi-layered | A sudden final reversal |
| Central technique | Architecture as a tool of deduction | Children's language as a conduit for clues |
| Greatest surprise | The architect's secret passages | The "true culprit" is the victim's protector |
| Moral register | A symmetrical battle between geniuses | An asymmetrical drama of sacrifice and love |

**Sholmes vs. Lupin: The Special Nature of This Confrontation**

Leblanc does something in this book unprecedented in the history of detective fiction: he allows two of the strongest "adversaries" to share the same book's central narrative axis, yet never lets either side win a complete victory. Sholmes solves nearly every mystery, recovers the blue diamond, and arrests Lupin; but Lupin escapes after his arrest and makes Ganimard look ridiculous, Clotilde's identity is personally sealed by Sholmes, and Sholmes's true "final failure" comes in "The Jewish Lamp" — his investigation succeeds, yet it destroys a family, exposes the truth about Bresson, and leaves the baroness's secret with nowhere to hide. Lupin, meanwhile, casually observes in the final scene: "If you had not intervened, I could have tidied everything up for them myself, without destroying anything."

This is a profound literary irony: **the detective's work is to reveal the truth, but sometimes the revelation of truth itself is the greatest harm.**

**The Writing Strategy Behind "Herlock Sholmes"**

Leblanc's treatment of Conan Doyle's original character combines homage with playful mockery. The partner he assigns to Sholmes — Wilson (a surrogate for Watson) — is a far more comedic foil: not only does he come to grief through various misjudgments, but he is truly wounded on two separate occasions (a broken arm; a knife wound near the heart). Conan Doyle later reportedly requested that Leblanc change the character's name to avoid confusion among readers, which is why the book uses "Herlock Sholmes" rather than "Sherlock Holmes." Yet Leblanc's portrayal of Sholmes is by no means malicious mockery — he allows the English detective to display genuine intelligence and character; he simply confronts him with an adversary more complex than any criminal: **a Lupin who possesses emotion, aesthetics, and a code of honor.**

**A Supplementary Note for Readers in Taiwan**

In "The Blonde Lady," the historical background of the blue diamond is worth mentioning in the Chinese-language context: the blue diamond once belonged to the French royal family (in the story's setting it is connected to the "Duc d'Orléans" and an actress). The rarity of such a gemstone lies in the fact that its natural blue color derives from the special lattice structure of boron atoms within diamond crystal — it is one of the rarest colors a diamond can possess, and the historically famous "Hope Diamond" is perhaps the most celebrated example. Leblanc's use of this as a prop aptly reflects the fascination of French upper-class society of the time with aristocratic relics. For the reader, the blue diamond is a symbol of "calcified social hierarchy," and Lupin's act of "stealing from the rich to serve the clever" thereby carries even greater moral tension.
