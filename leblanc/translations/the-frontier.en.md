# *The Frontier* — Deducibility Analysis

## Basic Information
- Author: Maurice Leblanc
- Year: 1912 (French original *La Frontière*; English translation published the same year)
- Translator: Alexander Teixeira de Mattos
- Type: Full-length political psychological thriller
- Detective: No fixed detective; old Morestal reasons by passion and intuition; Philippe is the central figure forced to "investigate himself"
- Core mystery type: whodunit (designer of the trap) + whydunit (motive behind false testimony) + contested truth (the Franco-German dispute over the site of arrest)

---

## Deducibility Rating

| Metric | Stars | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------|
| Deducibility | ⭐⭐ | Average convergence point approximately 30% — readers gain a privileged vantage from early on and grasp the core truths well ahead of the characters; the suspense lies not in "who did it" but in "what will it cost" |
| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐ | Moderate misdirection: Dourlowski's double identity, the layers of diplomatic lies, and the compounding cover Philippe erects over his false testimony together weave a dense net of fog |

> **Special Note:** *The Frontier* is Leblanc's most literarily ambitious work, entirely unlike the clever escapades of the Arsène Lupin series. The "mystery" here is not a locked room or a theft, but rather: can a person lie in order to prevent a war? And who bears the cost of that lie? Any analysis of the deductive dimension must take into account the fundamental gap between the **reader's privileged perspective** and the **internal perspective of the characters**.

---

## Synopsis

The Franco-German border, the Vosges Mountains. Old Morestal is a stubborn patriot who witnessed the humiliation of defeat in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870; since then he has fortified his border home and kept himself armed, waiting for the day of reconquest. His son Philippe is a history professor in Paris who nominally inherits his father's convictions, yet has quietly transformed into a passionate anti-war pacifist, anonymously writing an incendiary pamphlet titled *Peace Above All*.

Philippe returns to the family border home with his wife Marthe, intending to confront his father — to reveal his changed beliefs and his plan to resign his professorship. Suzanne, the daughter of border inspector Jorancé, happens to be staying nearby at the same time; she is deeply in love with Philippe, which sets off an entanglement.

On Monday evening, old Morestal and Jorancé are returning home along a border path when they fall into a trap engineered by German agents: a German soldier who claims to want to defect lures the two men into offering assistance, whereupon German police arrest them near the border. The crux of the matter is: did the arrest take place on French soil or German soil?

At this critical moment, Philippe has an alibi — that night he was with Suzanne, not near the border. To protect Suzanne's reputation, he chooses to fabricate testimony, claiming he "heard" Jorancé's protests. But as the diplomatic storm intensifies, Philippe's lies begin to crumble, and in the end everything is exposed one by one inside a tent where a joint Franco-German inquiry is held. The sound of guns on the border, and Philippe's final struggle between personal conviction and national instinct, form the novel's last, haunting note.

## Truth Breakdown

### Truth One: The Author of the Anonymous Pamphlet *Peace Above All*

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 30%**
- Answer: Philippe Morestal — history professor in Paris, son of old Morestal

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Old Morestal produces a purple-covered pamphlet and says "the author's name is Morestal… a distant relative, I suppose," whereupon Philippe immediately goes "pale" and asks "Are you sure? How did you find out?" | ~14% | The tone and reaction already betray the identity outright; an attentive reader can confirm it here |
| 2 | Philippe confesses to Marthe: "Those pamphlets I wrote anonymously… I've had the manuscript for a year but kept putting off handing it to a publisher" | ~17% | A direct admission; the truth is now fully visible |
| 3 | Old Morestal mentions a misdirected letter bearing the words "Congratulations on the pamphlet's success, my dear Morestal" — a letter originally addressed to Philippe in Paris | ~14% | Physical evidence: the misdirected letter confirms Philippe is the author |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Philippe's second book, *The Concept of the Fatherland*, sparks controversy; old Morestal writes to reproach him: "You seem to think that patriotism is only a passing phase of civilisation" | ~7% | Establishes the trajectory of Philippe's thought, hinting that he has already broken with his father's worldview |
| 2 | Philippe plans to resign, on the grounds that "I have no right to teach children what I now believe" | ~18% | Consistent with the logic of his anonymous writing; further confirms his double life |
| 3 | On old Morestal's bookshelf, Philippe's books are covered in dense, angry pencil annotations | ~9% | A symbolic detail: the rift between father and son runs all the way into the pages themselves |

#### Red Herrings

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | Old Morestal says "he seems to be named Morestal as well… some distant relative, the family is large" | ~14% | Leads first-time readers to assume it is some unknown remote cousin rather than the Philippe standing right there |
| 2 | Philippe claims "as a professor he would not need to serve in the army during wartime… he would serve in a government office" | ~82% | Creates the illusion that he will not go to the front; yet in the end he picks up a rifle and joins the defenders |

### Truth Two: The Designers of the Trap — Dourlowski and Weisslicht

- **Clue convergence point: approximately 43%**
- Answer: German inspector Weisslicht planned the operation; Dourlowski (pretending to be an intelligence informant for the French side, in reality a double agent) carried it out. They used the German soldier Baufeld as bait, premeditated the scene of Morestal and Jorancé "assisting a deserter" near the border, and thereby arranged the arrest of the French inspector.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Dourlowski secretly retrieves from Morestal's wastepaper basket the crumpled slip of paper on which the escape route is written | ~22% | **The single most important clue** — readers witness this act directly, and know the key prop of the trap has been secured |
| 2 | Marthe mentions that someone has seen Dourlowski in the company of German inspector Weisslicht | ~22% | The first indirect revelation of Dourlowski's double identity |
| 3 | Jorancé tells Morestal: "Dourlowski has not been working for us for six months… he has probably gone over to the other side" | ~32% | Formally confirms Dourlowski's defection |
| 4 | Old Morestal reasons out: "Baufeld's escape was a premeditated lure… Dourlowski had that slip of paper in his hand… he led Jorancé and me into the trap" | ~43% | Morestal's complete reconstruction of the mechanism; the entire trap is fully exposed here |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Dourlowski claims "I always go by the Devil's Pass and the factory road," yet asks Morestal to draw him an alternative route | ~22% | Implies he already knows the old route; asking for a new one is to furnish it to the German soldier |
| 2 | The farmer Saboureux has seen Weisslicht and Dourlowski walking and hiding together in the forest | ~52% | The geographical contact corroborates their conspiracy |
| 3 | German investigators refuse to acknowledge Saboureux's testimony; later Saboureux retracts it after a German newspaper publishes the story | ~53% | Demonstrates how the entire incident is twisted by political pressure on all sides |

#### Red Herrings

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | Dourlowski presents himself as a jovial itinerant pedlar, speaking patriotically about helping a German soldier "defect to France" | ~6%, ~22% | Leads both readers and Morestal to see him as merely a glib go-between rather than a mastermind |
| 2 | Old Morestal refuses to assist Dourlowski and crumples the slip of paper into the wastepaper basket — which seems to have cut off the danger | ~22% | Makes readers believe the threat has been neutralised; in reality Dourlowski immediately retrieves the paper from the basket |

### Truth Three: Philippe's Whereabouts That Night — He Was with Suzanne, Not Near the Border

- **Clue convergence point (reader's perspective): approximately 32%**
- **Clue convergence point (in-story characters' perspective): approximately 73%**
- Answer: On the night Morestal and Jorancé are arrested, Philippe stays at the Jorancé home in Saint-Élophe and spends the night with Suzanne, not returning until dawn. His alibi directly means that his initial testimony is a lie.

#### Key Clues (visible to the reader)

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Suzanne confesses her feelings to Philippe before the Aphrodite statue in the garden and allows him to kiss her | ~32% | Readers witness the kiss directly; Philippe then remains at Saint-Élophe and is not near the border |
| 2 | Philippe's bed is unslept in the next morning, proving he never returned to his room | ~38% | Physical evidence; readers already know the reason, but this is the signal that starts Marthe's suspicions |
| 3 | Suzanne pleads with Philippe: "In the inquiry, you won't be forced to confess, will you?" | ~56% | Confirms from Suzanne's own lips that something requiring secrecy has occurred between them |

#### Supporting Clues (the trail of suspicion among the characters)

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Marthe notices that Philippe's reaction to Suzanne in public is "too agitated" | ~36% | Marthe is the sharpest observer in the novel; her suspicions lag behind the reader's, but they point in exactly the right direction |
| 2 | Philippe says "I was in the vicinity… but I didn't see anything," pausing "for one second" as he speaks | ~42% | A hairline crack in his testimony; Marthe notices that his phrasing is "less certain" than in his first statement |
| 3 | Marthe's interrogation — "You didn't sleep at the old mill… so where were you?" — closes in step by step like a cross-examination | ~66–73% | Marthe's questioning draws all the contradictions together into one question that cannot be evaded |

#### Red Herrings

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | Suzanne lies to Marthe, saying "Philippe took the direct road home" | ~38% | Tries to help Philippe maintain his story, but makes the lie more fragile |
| 2 | Philippe says "I was so tired I fell asleep at once and heard nothing — no gunshots at all" | ~39% | Sounds plausible, yet directly contradicts the fact that his bed was never slept in |

### Truth Four: In Which Country Did the Arrest Actually Take Place?

- **Clue convergence point: unresolved throughout the entire novel**
- Answer: The author deliberately keeps this ambiguous, but readers have ample reason to believe that **old Morestal's memory is correct** — the arrest occurred on French soil. Yet this truth is buried under politics, contradictory testimony, and pressure from both sides, and in the end becomes a political fact determined by "who can convince whom" rather than an objective one.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Old Morestal says: "I have lived on this land for forty years; I know every signpost and every turning so well that I cannot be mistaken" | ~34% | His local knowledge is the most powerful subjective evidence |
| 2 | German investigators drive in stakes during the inquiry, claiming the arrest took place twenty yards east of the border line — but Morestal and Jorancé's testimony unanimously denies this | ~53% | Both sides insist on their version; the truth becomes a political bargaining chip |
| 3 | Old Morestal returns to the scene on Friday morning and asks himself: "What if I drifted off course?… Suppose I veered to the right…" — but then immediately dismisses the thought | ~55% | That momentary self-doubt is one of the most honest passages in the entire novel; Philippe seizes upon this instant to question his father's certainty |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Saboureux initially testifies that he saw Weisslicht and Dourlowski scheming in the forest together, then later retracts out of fear of war | ~52–54% | Shows how political forces can nullify evidence itself |
| 2 | The German soldier Baufeld tells Philippe on his deathbed that they were framed, and mentions the escape route | ~40% | Indirectly supports the French position, but Germany refuses to credit the testimony of a deserter |

#### Red Herrings

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | The Under-Secretary of State Lecorbier says "I almost wish you had been wrong" — hinting that "if France were a little off on the border line, it would actually be easier to resolve" | ~67% | Briefly makes readers wonder: perhaps old Morestal truly was mistaken after all? |

## Reasoning Path Reconstruction

**The Reader's Privileged Position and the Characters' State of Blindness**

The most striking narrative design of *The Frontier* is this: the reader possesses virtually all the important truths from the very beginning, yet can only watch helplessly as the characters march step by step toward self-destruction. This "tragedy of the omniscient perspective" forms a sharp contrast with the "triumph of the detective perspective" in traditional mystery fiction.

**Path One: Understanding the Trap (Whodunit)**

Around the 22% mark (Chapter Five, "The Note"), readers witness with their own eyes Dourlowski stealing the slip of paper from the wastepaper basket. This is one of the single most important scenes in the entire novel — readers "know" that a trap is about to spring, while old Morestal remains completely unaware. When the arrest occurs late on Monday night, readers have long been prepared to understand how that crumpled paper was put to use. Old Morestal's reconstruction of the plot (around 43%) merely "fills in" a puzzle that readers have already assembled seven or eight tenths of the way.

**Path Two: Tracking Philippe's Lie (Whydunit)**

Around the 32% mark, readers directly witness Philippe's embrace with Suzanne, and follow him as he walks back to the old mill at dawn and discovers the dying Baufeld. Philippe then lies and claims he was up and out early that morning; readers see through the lie at once and begin anxiously watching him sink ever deeper into the quicksand of false testimony.

**Path Three: The Transformation of Philippe's Motive for Perjury**

After roughly the 57% mark, the motive behind Philippe's lie undergoes a shift: initially he lied to protect Suzanne's reputation; but as the diplomatic crisis escalates, he begins actively "withdrawing" his testimony, attempting to dismantle his father's declaration and give the French government a way out. This is a masterful design in which a lie is re-purposed for a new objective — readers must keep up with the accumulation of motives, and understand that at this stage Philippe is not deploying a lie but rather using the "retraction of a lie" in an attempt to avert war.

**Path Four: Marthe's Investigation**

Among all the characters in the novel, Marthe is the one who comes closest to the role of detective. She uses no external resources whatsoever — only precise observation and remorseless logic: "You say you slept, but the bed is made. You say you heard no gunshots, yet you admit you were in the area at the time. You say you were going to talk to Father, but you kept putting it off." She layers all the contradictions together, and in the end inside the inquiry tent forces out the one question that cannot be avoided: "Then where were you?"

## Additional Notes

**Leblanc's Least-Leblanc Work**

*The Frontier* was published in 1912, on the very eve of the gathering storm clouds over Europe. By then the Arsène Lupin series was enormously popular, yet Leblanc chose in this novel to abandon entirely the elegant gentleman-thief hero, and instead wrote about an ordinary man torn apart among belief, love, family obligation, and national destiny. This is Leblanc's most literarily ambitious full-length work, and one of his least discussed.

**"Lies" as Narrative Engine**

In this novel, lies are not criminal acts but solutions to ethical dilemmas — yet every lie creates a deeper predicament than the one it tried to resolve. Philippe lies to protect Suzanne; the lie deepens the diplomatic crisis; retracting the lie requires sacrificing Suzanne. The novel shows how lies snowball, growing larger and larger until no one can walk away innocent and unscathed.

| Lie | Purpose | Cost |
|-----|---------|------|
| "I went straight home" | Conceal that he was with Suzanne | Generates contradictions in his alibi |
| "I heard Jorancé's protests" | Protect his father, stabilise the diplomatic situation | Forces him to become a diplomatic witness, increasing his responsibility |
| "I know nothing — I wasn't there" | Attempt to dismantle his father's testimony to prevent war | Exposes his wife's suspicions and triggers a domestic crisis |
| "It was a woman…" (implied) | Provide an alternative explanation without naming names | Seen through simultaneously by Marthe and Jorancé; the situation collapses |

**The Politicisation of Evidence: When Proof Becomes a Bargaining Chip**

Another point of deductive interest in *The Frontier* is that all physical evidence is "swallowed" by political purpose. The slip of paper that Dourlowski retrieved from the wastepaper basket could have served as ironclad proof of the trap — but it is also proof that old Morestal was "assisting a deserter." Saboureux's eyewitness account could prove the trap existed — but he retracts it out of fear of war. The ground position of the arrest site is a "objective fact" jointly claimed by both France and Germany — but no neutral survey can resolve the dispute. All of this constitutes a reminder more brutal than any ordinary detective case: evidence only carries meaning before a power that is willing to believe it.

**Comparison with Leblanc's Other Works**

| | Arsène Lupin Series | *The Frontier* |
|--|--|--|
| Protagonist's character | Calm, sharp-witted, unflappable | Philippe: sincere, torn, frequently losing control |
| Type of puzzle | Theft, identity mystery, mechanical trick | Political trap, diplomatic lie, crisis of conscience |
| Narrative tension | Reader and detective pursue the mystery together | Reader is ahead of the characters, witnessing tragedy unfold |
| Tone of resolution | Heroic triumph (or an escape with a smile) | Tragic compromise — a war that might have been averted begins |
| The nature of "truth" | Objectively existing, waiting to be uncovered | Subjectively constructed, shaped by political force |

**Philippe's Final "I'm Coming, Father"**

The last scene of the novel shows Philippe abandoning his plan to flee to Switzerland, picking up a rifle and kneeling at his father's side, firing at the German troops crossing the border. He says "I'm coming, father" — this is the most important sentence in the entire novel, and the most complex. He has not changed his convictions; he still believes this war is stupid and cruel. But he stays — not because he was wrong, but because "there are some things that even you yourself cannot refuse."

Leblanc has Philippe, in the novel's final line, kill a man — "a big man in uniform, who slowly collapsed, as if going to sleep." That is not the gesture of a hero; it is the tears of one who has been defeated. The real truth is that this war made everyone lose.
