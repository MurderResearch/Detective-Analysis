# The Incredible Theft — Detective Analysis

## Basic Information
- Author: Agatha Christie
- Year: 1937 (collected in *Murder in the Mews*; rewritten from the 1923 short story *The Submarine Plans*)
- Type: Short story (long-form short / novella)
- Detective: Hercule Poirot
- Core mystery type: whodunit + whydunit (dual mystery: identity of the thief + motive for the crime)

---

| Metric | Rating | Notes |

| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | Highly misleading: a triple-suspect shield (Mrs. Vanderlyn → Reggie → Carlile) layers concealment over the true perpetrator; the host's role as both "client and victim" completely deflects suspicion away from him |

## Synopsis

Lord Mayfield (born Sir Charles McLaughlin), Britain's Minister of Armaments, hosts a weekend gathering at his country estate. The guests include Air Marshal Sir George Carrington and his wife Lady Julia (a nervous, compulsive gambler), their son Reggie (a frivolous twenty-one-year-old), MP Mrs. Macatta, and the most pivotal guest: American visitor Mrs. Vanderlyn — a woman who moves among military and political figures of various nations by means of her beauty and has long been suspected of espionage.

That evening, Lord Mayfield asks his private secretary Carlile to organize classified documents in the study, among them the complete blueprints for Britain's newest bomber. After dinner, he invites Sir George for a walk on the terrace; the female guests retire upstairs one by one, and Mrs. Vanderlyn briefly returns to the drawing room on the pretext of fetching a book. When the two men return indoors, the classified blueprints have vanished from the study. Lord Mayfield claims he personally witnessed a figure fleeing through the study window from the terrace.

Hercule Poirot is called in to investigate. He systematically questions all the guests: Carlile says he heard the maid Léonie cry out in the corridor, while Léonie claims she encountered a ghost; Mrs. Vanderlyn admits she came downstairs to fetch a book and then went up to have her maid brush her hair; Lady Julia also admits to going downstairs twice; Reggie claims he spent the entire evening in his room reading a detective novel, and is not even sure whether he heard any screaming.

Poirot strips away the disguises on each clue one by one: there are no footprints on the grass outside the study window, ruling out an outside intruder; if Carlile had wanted to steal the blueprints, he could have simply copied them at any time — there was no need to stage such a clumsy burglary; the "figure at the window" rests solely on Mayfield's own testimony. The most dramatic breakthrough comes from the maid Léonie — from the way she moves her hands when describing her hair being brushed, Poirot deduces that the supposed "ghost encounter" was merely Léonie being startled by Reggie suddenly embracing her, thereby establishing Reggie's whereabouts: he never touched the documents.

After eliminating everyone else, the only person remaining is Lord Mayfield himself.

Poirot ultimately reveals the full picture: years ago, Mayfield had secretly approached a foreign power and engaged in informal negotiations, afterward being forced to publicly deny it — but the other party retained a dangerously worded letter. Now, as Mayfield stands on the threshold of becoming Prime Minister, the foreign power uses the letter as leverage, demanding the blueprints of Britain's newest bomber in exchange for its return. Mrs. Vanderlyn is the intermediary. Mayfield personally "stole" the blueprints he was responsible for safeguarding, handing them over during the terrace walk; in return, she delivered the fatal letter to him disguised as an "unsent personal letter."

## Truth Breakdown

### Truth One: Identity of the Thief — Lord Mayfield Himself (whodunit)

- **Convergence point: approximately 84%**
- Answer: Lord Mayfield personally took the blueprints from the desk and concealed them in his pocket, then falsely claimed he had watched a figure escape through the study window from the terrace.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | There are no footprints or marks of any kind on the grass outside the study window | ~30% | Directly eliminates the possibility of an outside intruder, establishing that the theft was an inside job — all subsequent reasoning must focus entirely on those within the house |
| 2 | Lord Mayfield has poor eyesight (nearsighted), yet his description of "the figure fleeing through the window" is strangely definite and unhesitating | ~42% | Poirot explicitly points out this contradiction: a nearsighted person could not possibly be so certain about a figure in the dark unless he fabricated the sighting; this very certainty is the crack in the lie |
| 3 | Lord Mayfield is desperate to vouch for Carlile's innocence, declaring he would "stake his reputation" on Carlile's loyalty | ~30% | Poirot notes that if Carlile had intended to steal, he had no need whatsoever to stage any "burglary scene" — he could have copied the documents at any time without anyone knowing. Mayfield's eagerness to exonerate him inadvertently turns the suspicion back on himself |
| 4 | Mayfield's stated reason for inviting Mrs. Vanderlyn — that he wished to set a trap to expose her — is extremely weak, as he has no concrete plan whatsoever | ~50% | A seasoned political strategist who truly intended to lure out a spy would have a substantive plan; this hole-riddled explanation is fundamentally a cover story |
| 5 | Léonie confesses: her "ghost cry" was simply from being startled by Reggie suddenly embracing her; Poirot thereby confirms Reggie had no contact with the study all evening | ~84% | This is the final piece of the elimination puzzle. At this point, all other suspects have been cleared: no footprints for an outside intruder (eliminated), Carlile had no logical need to steal (eliminated), Mrs. Vanderlyn has her maid as witness (eliminated), Sir George was on the terrace with Mayfield (eliminated), Mrs. Macatta's snoring was heard by the neighboring room (eliminated), Reggie and Léonie were together on the staircase (eliminated) — only Mayfield remains |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Sir George confirms he was with Mayfield on the terrace the entire time, walking back and forth at least five or six times | ~30% | Appears to be Mayfield's alibi, but in fact reveals another possibility: the terrace connects to the outside of the study window, and during their walks back and forth the two men had multiple opportunities to approach the study window — the desk sits right by the window, within arm's reach |
| 2 | Lady Julia goes downstairs twice during the night and lingers in the corridor | ~75% | Her suspicious movements are later explained (she went to check on her son's room), but in the process effectively draws the reader's attention toward her son |
| 3 | Mayfield displays intense anxiety about "innocent persons falling under suspicion," repeatedly insisting that as long as the documents are recovered, he does not care who took them | Throughout | Poirot notes this reflects that Mayfield's "conscience is not entirely dead" — he does not want Carlile or other innocent parties to suffer for his actions; this psychology indirectly signals that he acted under compulsion rather than by nature |
| 4 | Mrs. Vanderlyn is "in excellent spirits" the morning after the theft, composed during questioning, and departs calmly at the end | ~84–96% | She has already received the blueprints she needed; her mission is accomplished, so there is no reason to be nervous; this ease creates a subtle contrast with the caution one would expect from "a suspected spy" |

#### False Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | Mrs. Vanderlyn is treated as a "suspicious person" by everyone (including the host) from the very beginning, and she genuinely had the opportunity to access the documents (she came downstairs to fetch a book) | ~10–30% | Readers naturally tend to assume she is the thief herself, or at least personally carried out the theft — whereas the truth is that she was only the recipient, not the one who committed the theft |
| 2 | Reggie's obvious infatuation with Mrs. Vanderlyn, combined with Lady Julia discovering her son's room empty during the night | ~70–85% | Strongly implies Reggie was seduced by her beauty into stealing on her behalf; Lady Julia believes this version so thoroughly that she is prepared to "return the blueprints on her son's behalf," nearly leading the reader to accept this explanation entirely |
| 3 | Carlile, as the insider with the closest access to the documents, becomes the object of Poirot's questioning and the implied suspicion of the assembled company | ~30–45% | Readers instinctively regard "the person with the greatest opportunity" as the primary suspect, while Mayfield's public championing of Carlile makes readers feel the host is simply "protecting a loyal subordinate" |
| 4 | Léonie's "ghost" story and Lady Julia's "white-clad figure" in the corridor | ~45–75% | Creates a complex nocturnal mystery in the hallway, drawing the reader's attention toward the maid, the mistress, and the midnight corridor — far away from the true protagonist in the study |

### Truth Two: Motive — Political Blackmail and a Secret Exchange (whydunit)

- **Convergence point: approximately 95%**
- Answer: Five years earlier, Mayfield had secretly approached a European foreign power and conducted informal negotiations. He was subsequently forced to publicly deny it, but the other party retained a dangerously worded private letter as leverage. Seizing on the moment when Mayfield stands to become Prime Minister, the foreign power threatens to make the letter public unless he hands over the blueprints of Britain's newest bomber in exchange. Mayfield chooses his personal future over national secrets, and Mrs. Vanderlyn serves as the intermediary to complete the exchange.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Five years earlier, Mayfield caused a public controversy by his close association with a "European power unpopular with the British public," and the Prime Minister subsequently issued a public clarification on his behalf | ~62% | This is the historical root of the motive. Poirot raises this during his interview with Mayfield, who acknowledges it but appears puzzled — why bring up old business? That puzzlement is itself a signal |
| 2 | Poirot advances a "political sabotage" theory: someone may have engineered the theft to damage Mayfield's reputation | ~62–70% | Mayfield's reaction is "Absurd!" — but the vehemence of his reaction draws Poirot's attention; Poirot has the direction right but the subject wrong — Mayfield is not the one being sabotaged, he is the one doing it |
| 3 | "Ah, but I am the greatest fool! I am the most complete imbecile!" — Poirot suddenly has an epiphany after Mayfield casually mentions "I started from the bottom — I am a first-class engineer" | ~63% | Poirot's intuition clicks fully into place here: Mayfield, as a trained engineer, understands better than anyone what grade of secret he is handing over and what it is worth — this "full understanding of one's own actions" is a necessary condition for premeditation |
| 4 | Poirot's final revelation: the complete exchange scheme of letter for blueprints, and "a letter disguised as an unsent personal letter" as the means of delivery | ~97% | Only here is the full picture of the motive presented — the reader learns not just "who did it" but "the complete cause and effect of the entire political transaction" |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Notes |
|---|------|--------------|-------|
| 1 | Mayfield's abnormal anxiety about "innocent persons being implicated," and the guilty conscience he betrays throughout the investigation | Throughout | He is not a cold-blooded traitor but a man driven to the wall; this psychological undercurrent provides emotional support for "the struggle of a man being blackmailed" |
| 2 | Poirot points out that if news of the theft were to leak, combined with media insinuations about Mayfield's "relationship" with Mrs. Vanderlyn, it would be enough to destroy his path to the premiership at the most critical moment | ~65% | This reveals the pressure the other side is exerting: rather than let an old letter destroy his future, it is more rational to buy silence with blueprints — this exchange has a "rational" basis in terms of personal interest calculation |

#### False Clues

| # | Clue | Location (%) | Misdirection |
|---|------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | The "political sabotage" theory advanced by Poirot — someone may be using this affair to destroy Mayfield | ~62% | Leads readers to believe Mayfield is a victim being framed; in fact the direction is entirely reversed: he is not being sabotaged by someone else — he has personally betrayed the secrets he was entrusted to protect |

## Reasoning Path Reconstruction

**Path One: Elimination to Identify the Thief**

At approximately the 30% mark, attentive readers can deliver a decisive blow with "no footprints on the grass outside the window": the theft must be an inside job, and the possibility of an outside intruder is zero. The question then narrows to: which guest or servant?

At around 42%, "Mayfield is nearsighted yet describes the figure at the window with certainty" is already a glaring flaw — but most readers at this point have not yet connected it to Mayfield's own guilt. The easier reading is "he simply misremembered."

At around 50%, if readers catch the logical gap of "Mayfield invited Mrs. Vanderlyn but has no actual plan to trap her," they can begin to suspect his motives are not pure. This point is easy to overlook because it is an absence (no plan) rather than a presence (a concrete suspicion).

At around 75%, Lady Julia's nocturnal wanderings receive an explanation; at around 84%, Léonie's confession completely clears Reggie. At this moment the reader's elimination tally reads: no footprints for an outside intruder (eliminated), Carlile had no logical need to steal (eliminated), Mrs. Vanderlyn has her maid as witness (eliminated), Sir George was on the terrace with Mayfield (eliminated), Mrs. Macatta's snoring was heard by a neighbor (eliminated), Reggie and Léonie were together on the staircase (eliminated).

After all eliminations, only one person remains.

Mayfield claims he "was on the terrace with Sir George," but the two were walking back and forth rather than standing still on guard; the study window is at one end of the terrace, and during their walk he had ample opportunity to briefly turn back and reach through the window to take the blueprints from the desk. His claim to have seen "a figure fleeing through the window" was precisely designed to establish Carlile's innocence — the figure never existed; its only purpose was to make the incident look like an outside theft.

**Path Two: Reconstruction of Motive**

At around 62%, Poirot raises Mayfield's old political scandal; Mayfield acknowledges it but seems puzzled. Just as the interview is drawing to a close, Mayfield casually remarks "I started from the bottom — I am a first-class engineer" — at this instant Poirot has his epiphany ("Ah, but I am the greatest fool!"). For readers, this epiphany is a signal: Christie is indicating that Poirot already knows the truth, and clever readers should at this point go back and piece together all the clues, asking themselves: "Why would Mayfield's engineering background give Poirot an epiphany?"

The answer is: only a person who truly understands the value of the blueprints could carry out the precise negotiation of "blueprints in exchange for a letter." He was not passively duped but was actively negotiating — this transaction required an expert insider.

The complete motive is only spelled out by Poirot at the 97% mark, but for readers who catch the "Ah, but I am the greatest fool!" moment at 62% and think it through seriously, the outline of the answer can already be sketched.

## Additional Notes

**Christie's Cleverest Identity Disguise: The Client Is the Culprit**

The central design of this story lies in the fact that Mayfield simultaneously occupies three positions: **victim** (the blueprints were "stolen" from him), **host** (he provides the detective with every facility for the investigation), and **client** (he personally summoned Poirot). Readers instinctively place "the person who reports the crime" and "the culprit" at completely opposite ends of the spectrum — this instinctive bias is the very foundation on which Christie constructs the entire fog of the story.

The "client is the culprit" technique is not unique to Christie, but she executes it here with exceptional elegance: Mayfield's anxiety ("don't let innocent people fall under suspicion"), his championship of Carlile, his "full cooperation" with the investigation — all of these behaviors can be read equally as the conduct of a good man or as the carefully staged performance of a culprit. Both readings coexist until the very last moment.

**The Layered Onion Structure of Three Red Herrings**

This story showcases Christie's most skillful layered misdirection:

| Layer | Suspect | Cleared When |
|-------|---------|--------------|
| First layer (most conspicuous) | Mrs. Vanderlyn — foreign spy | Her maid testifies she was upstairs all evening |
| Second layer (next most conspicuous) | Reggie — young man seduced by a beautiful woman | Léonie confesses he was embracing her on the staircase |
| Third layer (most "logical") | Carlile — the insider with closest access to the documents | Poirot demonstrates he had no need to stage a theft |

With each layer peeled away, the reader thinks "now I've finally found it," only to discover yet another false lead. Christie gives every suspect ample reason to be suspected, ensuring the reader's attention keeps flowing in the wrong direction.

**The "Unreliable Eyewitness" as a Central Trick in Context**

Mayfield's central lie — "I saw with my own eyes a figure fleeing through the window" — belongs to the classic detective fiction technique of "fabricated witness testimony." Christie adds a superb additional design here: the eyewitness's natural disability of "poor eyesight" can be read in two ways simultaneously — "so he may simply have been mistaken" (providing a defense for the lie) or "so he could not possibly be that certain" (the real crack in the story). This dual interpretability is what makes the clue design so masterful.

**Comparing *The Incredible Theft* and *The Submarine Plans***

Christie rewrote *The Submarine Plans* (1923) in 1937 as *The Incredible Theft*; both stories center on "stolen military secrets" and both feature Poirot's intervention. The key differences are: the original has a relatively simple structure, while the rewrite substantially increases the complexity of character relationships (Lady Julia's gambling debts, Reggie's frivolity, Mayfield's political ambitions) and the depth of motive (from straightforward secret-stealing to a calculus of political blackmail and personal future). The rewritten version is far richer than the original in its "motivational layers," but as a result the convergence point for the whydunit clues is pushed much further back, producing a considerably higher level of deductive difficulty.

**A Note for Taiwanese Readers**

The political background of this story involves secret diplomacy between Britain and a certain European power in the 1930s — from the story's context, this almost certainly alludes to the "appeasement-minded" figures in British political circles in the period surrounding the rise of Nazi Germany. At that time, there genuinely existed a faction within British diplomatic circles who advocated making private contact with Germany to maintain European equilibrium; if such contacts were exposed, they were simultaneously against public opinion and regarded as treasonous, and so could only be officially handled as "events that never happened." Mayfield's predicament, against this historical backdrop, carries considerable real-world plausibility — he is not merely a fictional politician but a portrait of a certain type of real person from that era.
