# The Adventure of the Egyptian Tomb — Detective Analysis

## Basic Information
- Author: Agatha Christie
- Year: 1923 (collected in *Poirot Investigates*)
- Type: Short Story
- Detective: Hercule Poirot
- Core Mystery Type: whodunit + howdunit + whydunit

| Metric | Rating | Notes |

| Fog Rating | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ | The "pharaoh's curse" serves as the central smokescreen; four deaths with entirely different causes that cannot be linked; Hastings's mistaken theory, the nervous secretary, and Hassan's suspicious behavior add multiple layers of false leads |

## Synopsis

London, 1923. At an excavation site near the pharaoh's tomb in Giza, Egypt, a series of deaths erupts in quick succession: expedition leader Sir John Willard dies of heart failure, American financier and sponsor Mr. Bleibner succumbs to acute blood poisoning, and his nephew Rupert Bleibner in New York shoots himself dead, leaving a note that reads "I am a leper." The "Curse of Men-her-Ra" blazes across newspaper front pages, and the mystical power of ancient Egypt is whipped into a frenzy.

Lady Willard comes to Poirot for help, asking him to protect her son Sir Guy — a young man determined to carry on his father's work and continue the excavation. Poirot accepts the commission and declares his belief in supernatural forces, much to Hastings's astonishment. The two set off for Egypt.

On their very first day at the desert camp, more bad news arrives: Mr. Schneider, the representative of the American Metropolitan Museum, dies of tetanus. Four deaths, four entirely different causes, all within a month of the tomb's opening. Poirot questions the expedition members: Dr. Tosswill of the museum, team physician Dr. Ames, secretary Mr. Harper, and Hassan, the old family retainer of the Willards.

That night, a phantom of Anubis — the Egyptian god of the underworld — appears in the camp, staff in hand; those who give chase find nothing. Poirot ceremoniously draws protective runes around his tent and accepts a cup of camomile tea sent by Hassan. A moment later, word spreads that Poirot has collapsed inside his tent. Hastings rushes off to fetch Dr. Ames.

The two hurry back, only to find Poirot sitting up calmly — he had never drunk the tea, but poured it into a medicine bottle for preservation. At that instant, Dr. Ames realizes he has given himself away; he quickly puts something into his mouth, the air fills with the scent of bitter almonds, and he crashes to the ground.

Poirot announces: the murderer is Dr. Ames, who has three lives on his hands.

## Truth Breakdown

### Truth One: Identity of the Killer (whodunit)

- **Convergence Point: ~70%**
- Answer: Dr. Ames, the expedition's medical officer. He was an old friend of Rupert Bleibner and had known of the will ever since Rupert, in a drunken moment, drew up a jokingly-worded document — a will that gave Ames his motive for financial gain.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Rupert Bleibner claims "he has a good friend in Egypt who can lend him money," and he did indeed obtain money for his passage back to New York | ~20% | Had he meant his uncle, he would have simply said "uncle"; saying "good friend" implies someone else — later confirmed to be Ames, who had in fact lent him money |
| 2 | Hassan relays a warning that "there is evil here"; Poirot notes this is a warning, not an accusation | ~65% | The focus is not on Hassan himself, but on the inner circle he serves |
| 3 | Harper tells Poirot that Ames (and Schneider) were acquainted with Rupert Bleibner, not just with the elder Bleibner | ~55% | **The pivotal turn** — Poirot explicitly states "only a doctor could carry out and conceal these crimes," and this clue is what confirms his target |
| 4 | Ames's excessively dismissive attitude toward the supernatural curse — for a "man of science," his insistence is almost theatrical | ~45% | Contrasted with the unease of others, Ames's certainty is too deliberate; he knows it is not a curse because he himself arranged everything |
| 5 | Poirot stages his own collapse; when Ames is summoned and arrives, he immediately reaches to check — and then, cornered, takes poison on the spot | ~90% | The true guilty party's unmasking: an innocent person would not choose to poison themselves at this moment |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Ames had no tetanus antitoxin on hand and needed it fetched from Cairo | ~40% | A field physician fully aware of outdoor risks who lacks antitoxin is itself abnormal |
| 2 | When Dr. Tosswill accuses Poirot of being ignorant of ancient Egyptian superstitions, Ames reacts noticeably more intensely than anyone else | ~72% | He is the most eager for everyone to believe the curse narrative — because it is the smokescreen protecting him |
| 3 | Poirot observes that Harper is "completely on edge," but then says meaningfully, "Don't forget — he also has a victim back in New York" | ~60% | The subtext of Poirot's remark: his suspicion at this moment is no longer on Harper; he is covertly watching Ames's reaction |
| 4 | The Anubis phantom was in fact staged by Poirot on Hassan's instructions, as a test of whether the killer truly believes in the supernatural | ~80% | Ames rushes out to "catch the impostor" — precisely what Poirot means when he says "he was not frightened by my hocus-pocus" |

#### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Misleading Direction |
|---|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Mr. Harper's extreme nervousness and eagerness to leave Egypt | ~60% | Makes the reader suspect Harper is the killer; Poirot himself says "my suspicions were initially divided between Harper and Ames" |
| 2 | Hassan sends a cup of camomile tea | ~82% | Makes the reader suspect the loyal old retainer Hassan is Ames's accomplice (he is in fact an innocent intermediary) |
| 3 | Hastings's theory that "Rupert accidentally killed Sir John" | ~22% | A superficially coherent alternative explanation, misleading readers into thinking the uncle's death is the central mystery and that Rupert's suicide was motivated by remorse |

### Truth Two: Method of the Crimes (howdunit)

- **Convergence Point: ~85%**
- Answer: Ames used his medical knowledge to carry out three murders while hiding behind the "curse." He inoculated the elder Bleibner with blood-poisoning bacteria; he diagnosed his old friend Rupert with "leprosy" (exploiting Rupert's history of living on South Pacific islands to make the lie believable), driving him to despair and suicide; and he killed Schneider with tetanus bacteria.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Rupert's suicide note reads: "I am a leper" | ~20% | Everyone interprets this as a metaphor ("I am a social outcast"), but Rupert meant it literally — he believed he had contracted leprosy |
| 2 | Rupert had lived on South Pacific islands where leprosy was prevalent | ~20% | Establishes the conditions under which the lie was plausible: he had genuinely been exposed to the environment of the disease, and would be all the more susceptible to deception if he had any skin symptoms |
| 3 | Harper mentions that Rupert said "he thought there was something wrong with him, but it wasn't serious" | ~55% | Rupert did indeed have minor skin trouble — the very material Ames used to "diagnose leprosy" |
| 4 | The four deaths have entirely different causes (heart failure, blood poisoning, suicide, tetanus) — Poirot explicitly identifies this as the crux of the matter | ~42% | The inverse logic: the variety of causes is by deliberate design; different methods prevent any connection being drawn, yet make the "curse" seem all the more mysterious |
| 5 | As expedition physician, Ames has both the opportunity and the technical skill to inoculate patients with disease-causing bacteria | Throughout | Only a doctor could make the act of inoculation invisible within a normal medical examination framework |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Ames confirms that the festering thumb wound of the elder Bleibner is "blood poisoning," not tetanus — the two are similar in presentation but entirely distinct | ~40% | Ames's medical precision is a performance to convince the reader, but he is also the only one who could precisely control the cause of death |
| 2 | The bitter almond smell of the camomile tea hints at cyanide | ~90% | Ames had poisoned the tea in an attempt to kill Poirot — a method consistent with the earlier murders: using an everyday medium to deliver poison |

#### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Misleading Direction |
|---|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1 | The "supernatural curse" framework surrounding all four deaths | Throughout | Draws readers into searching for a logic behind the pharaoh's curse, rather than asking "who has the medical capability to produce multiple different causes of death" |
| 2 | Sir John's heart failure is counted among the "curse victims" | ~10% | But Poirot explicitly says "I can see no one who could benefit from Sir John's death" — this is the only natural death; Ames merely exploited this fortuitous event |

### Truth Three: Motive — Inheritance (whydunit)

- **Convergence Point: ~97%**
- Answer: Years earlier, Rupert Bleibner drew up a will in a drunken, joking spirit, bequeathing all his possessions to "my good friend Robert Ames." After the elder Bleibner's death, his estate passed by intestacy to his nephew Rupert, and from Rupert (now dead) to the will's beneficiary, Ames. The entire scheme was designed to channel the money into Ames's hands.

#### Key Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | Harper says: "As far as we know, Mr. Bleibner did not make a will" | ~55% | On the surface a legal fact; in reality, it is the precondition for Ames to inherit from Rupert — without the elder Bleibner leaving a will, the estate passes to his nephew |
| 2 | The elder Bleibner shouts at his nephew: "Not a penny of my money will you ever have, living or dead. I intend to leave my money to further the work I've given my life to" | ~55% | Confirms the estate would not go directly to Rupert upon the elder Bleibner's death; Ames's scheme must take a detour — kill the elder Bleibner (estate passes by intestacy to Rupert), then cause Rupert's death (will passes to Ames) |
| 3 | Rupert does in fact obtain money for his passage back to New York, indicating someone lent it to him — and that someone is Ames | ~20% | This clue appears early in the story yet is nearly impossible to read for meaning at this point; in retrospect, however, it is evidence of the timing of Ames's actions |
| 4 | The drunken, joking will (revealed in full) | ~97% | **The decisive clue that appears almost at the story's very end**: "I leave my cigarette case… and everything of which I die possessed to my good friend Robert Ames" |

#### Supporting Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Notes |
|---|------|-------------|-------|
| 1 | The elder Bleibner is enormously wealthy, the financial backer of the expedition | ~15% | Establishes that the inheritance is large enough to serve as a motive for murder |
| 2 | Ames is Rupert's old friend (who once saved his life) | ~55% (Poirot's deduction) → 97% (confirmed by will) | Explains why a dissolute drifter would leave his possessions to a doctor friend |

#### False Clues

| # | Clue | Position (%) | Misleading Direction |
|---|------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1 | The elder Bleibner declares he intends to leave his money to the museum's work, and had discussed this with Schneider | ~55% | Leads readers to suspect Schneider may be a person of interest in the contest for the inheritance; in fact this is merely interference noise within Ames's plan |

## Reconstruction of the Reasoning Path

**Path One: From "Good Friend" to Killer**

At around the 20% mark of the story, there is a detail easily overlooked: Rupert makes his way from the South Pacific to Egypt and says he has "a good friend in Egypt who can lend him money" — but this is not his uncle, who has already refused him to his face. A careful reader who at this point asks "who is that person?" will, by the 55% mark when Harper mentions that Ames knew Rupert, be able to connect the clues: Ames is the "good friend" who lent him money.

But this alone is not enough to convict. What truly solidifies suspicion is a combination of three elements: first, only a doctor could inoculate patients with pathogens without arousing suspicion (professional capability); second, Ames's repeated insistence that "science cannot explain the supernatural" sounds too much like a performance amid the general atmosphere of panic (psychological detail); third, Poirot sets the Anubis trap (around 75%), and Ames's calm reaction — not the fear or awe of the others, but an immediate rush to "catch the person" — confirms he knows the phantom is fake, because the entire "curse" charade is his own arrangement. A reader who grasps all three points can reasonably point to Ames by around the 70% mark.

**Path Two: Reading "I Am a Leper" Literally**

This is the story's most refined piece of clue-craft. At the 20% mark, Hastings quotes the wording of Rupert's suicide note: "I am a leper." Everyone — including Hastings, the reader, and even the police — reads this as a metaphor: "I am a social outcast, less than human." But Christie is playing a game of linguistic reversal: what is said literally is what is true.

To read the correct meaning at this point, a reader must simultaneously notice three conditions: (1) Rupert genuinely lived on South Pacific islands where leprosy was prevalent; (2) he had "minor skin trouble" on his person (around 55%); (3) his "good friend" is a doctor with the authority to make diagnoses. Only when all three overlap does it become visible that Ames manipulated Rupert's mind with a false diagnosis. This path converges at around 55%, but requires readers to actively resist the metaphorical lure of the language.

**Path Three: Tracking the Will**

This path is nearly impossible for a reader to deduce independently within the story. The will is a drunken joke, described in passing, and only revealed in an epilogue at the very end. The sole preceding hint is: Rupert "didn't know if there was a will or not" — this appears at the 55% mark and could, technically, prompt a reader to ask "if there is a beneficiary, who is it?" But there are no subsequent clues to support this line of thinking. This layer of truth is the final piece of the puzzle Christie reserves for Poirot; readers have almost no chance of getting there before he does.

## Additional Remarks

**The Curse as a Narrative Weapon**

*The Adventure of the Egyptian Tomb* was written in 1923, just months after the sensational worldwide news of the opening of Tutankhamun's tomb that same year. Newspapers were full of reports about the "pharaoh's curse," and Christie used this as her raw material to craft a story about "using real social panic as a cover for crime." She understood that the era's belief in the supernatural was itself the perfect accomplice: if you could make people believe that death came from the gods, then even if you killed someone in broad daylight, eyewitnesses would attribute it to the curse. This is murder in a sociological sense — and Christie's most razor-sharp dissection of mass hysteria.

**Poirot's Strategy of Feigned Belief**

Poirot's core tactic in this story is to deliberately pretend to believe in the supernatural — carrying a copy of *Egyptian Magic*, drawing pentagrams outside his tent, performing a role of being frightened by the curse. This performance serves two purposes: first, to lull the killer into thinking Poirot has been blinded by superstition and is no longer a threat; second, to test Ames's reaction — if he shows no genuine fear in the face of the mysterious display, it confirms he knows the whole business is man-made.

Hastings, for a time, believes Poirot genuinely believes in the supernatural, and this itself is another layer of fog Christie lays over the reader: by exploiting the narrator's misunderstanding, she leads readers to misread the detective's true intentions.

**The Asymmetric Structure of Four Deaths**

Christie designs a compelling asymmetry: of the four deaths, only three are murders — and the very death that gave rise to the "curse" story, Sir John's heart failure, is the one truly natural death. Killer Ames did not kill Sir John; that was coincidence, a gift from fortune, which allowed him to insert his own crimes into a ready-made atmosphere of panic. This detail raises the puzzle's difficulty by one full degree: the detective is not looking for a killer among four murders, but distinguishing real from false in a fabricated scene where three murders and one accident are interwoven.

**Comparison with Christie's Contemporary Works**

That same year (1923), Christie published *Murder on the Links*, in which the judge's identity is the narrative core. By contrast, this short story — constrained by its length — sees Christie choose to forgo deep character development in favor of a plot mechanism in which "a single lie underpins an entire murder scheme." During this period she was feeling out the edges of her own style: the blurring of the literal and the metaphorical, social panic over the supernatural as a smokescreen, the doctor as the hardest suspect to accuse — all elements that would be developed in depth in her more mature works, such as *Murder on the Orient Express* and *And Then There Were None*.

**A Note for Readers**

The truth behind the "Tutankhamun curse" that this story drew upon remains debated to this day: members of the excavation team did indeed die in succession, with scientific explanations including fungal toxins, harmful gases accumulated in enclosed spaces, and simple death from old age and natural causes. Christie's insight was her recognition that "the power of the curse lies not in whether it is real or not, but in whether people believe it" — and that is the story's most modern proposition.
