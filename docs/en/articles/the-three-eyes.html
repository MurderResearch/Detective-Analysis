Three Eyes (The Three Eyes) — Deducibility Analysis

Basic Information

  • Author: Maurice Leblanc
  • Year: 1921
  • Type: Novel
  • Detective: None (the narrator is an eyewitness and participant; engineer Benjamin Prevotelle makes a guest appearance late in the story to reveal the solution)
  • Core mystery type: whatdunit (where do the images come from?) + whodunit (identity of the killer) + hidden truth (Berangere's true role and origins)

Deducibility Rating

Metric Rating Notes
Deducibility ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Average convergence point: 74% — the three truth threads are scattered; the hardest (Berangere's origins) does not surface until 95%; the central puzzle (Venus projection) requires astronomical intuition to deduce before Prevotelle's formal revelation
Fog Rating ⭐⭐⭐⭐ The author deliberately deploys three simultaneous layers of misdirection: multiple pseudo-scientific explanations, misreading of the dying man's last words, and Berangere's suspicious behavior; most unusually, in Chapter 3 the narrator openly announces "do not try to guess the riddle," using the metanarrative structure itself as a form of misdirection

Synopsis

In Haut-Meudon, a suburb of Paris, the inventor Noel Dorgeroux has discovered a remarkable phenomenon on a specially treated wall in his garden — a wall coated with a mysterious dark grey compound. At dusk, the wall projects vivid historical images of itself: the execution of British nurse Edith Cavell at the hands of the Germans, the first balloon flight of the Montgolfier brothers, the bombardment of Reims Cathedral, and even the moment of his own son Dominique's death in aerial combat.

Before each image appears, the wall first lights up with three strange geometric shapes — three "triangular eyes," each radiating the same expression as the eyes of the protagonist about to appear, as though announcing a preview.

Dorgeroux's nephew, Victorien Beaugrand, is the sole witness to these events. He has fallen in love with the old man's ward, Berangere. The inventor plans to build a circular amphitheater capable of holding a thousand spectators, intending to display this miracle to the public, and promises to let the two marry.

But a conspiracy to seize the secret has already taken shape in silence. A slippery man named Velmot spends the entire winter drawing close to Berangere and infiltrating the garden; his accomplice steals Dorgeroux's research notes and seizes a steel plate hidden in the lining of his waistcoat — the plate engraved with the core formula. Shortly thereafter, Dorgeroux is murdered in his own garden, Berangere vanishes, and control of the estate passes to a grinning stranger who claims to be Berangere's father: the merchant Theodore Massignac.

Massignac boldly opens the amphitheater, allowing the public to witness the wall's wonders. The young engineer Benjamin Prevotelle analyzes all the phenomena and ultimately announces in his report: the images are no fraud — they originate from Venus (the "Shepherd's Star," l'Etoile du Berger), projected by "gravitational rays" (B-rays). Intelligent life on Venus, using technology far beyond humanity's, is observing Earth and transmitting images to us.

Everything collapses at the third public showing: the wall projects the full sequence of Dorgeroux's murder, revealing the killer's face before a thousand witnesses. The enraged crowd tears the killer apart; with his dying breath he activates a device that douses the wall in corrosive liquid — the formula is lost forever. Berangere, who had memorized the formula, loses it in the trauma of her struggle with the criminals. Venus's signal never appears again.

Truth Analysis

Truth One: The Source of the Three-Eyes Images — Venus's Gravitational Rays

  • Convergence point: approximately 68%
  • Answer: Intelligent life on Venus (the "Shepherd's Star," l'Etoile du Berger) projects images of Earth's historical events from afar onto Dorgeroux's chemically coated wall, using "B-rays of gravitation" (which travel at three times the speed of light). The three geometric "eyes" are the Venusians' "producer's trademark," simultaneously displaying the eye-expression of the protagonist who is about to appear — because the Venusian physiology possesses three eyes and three arms in a "triangular symmetry," corresponding to humanity's "bilateral symmetry."

Truth Two: Dorgeroux's Killer — Massignac

  • Convergence point: approximately 58%
  • Answer: The merchant Theodore Massignac, who appears claiming to be Berangere's father, is the man who killed Dorgeroux with a pickaxe. He conspired with his accomplice Velmot: Velmot handled the theft of documents, attempted to produce images using stolen research fragments, and served as the getaway driver after the murder; Massignac employed the "last resort" of committing the crime himself, removing the steel plate formula from the lining of Dorgeroux's waistcoat.

Truth Three: Berangere's True Role and Origins — An Innocent Victim, Dorgeroux's Daughter

  • Convergence point: approximately 95%
  • Answer: Berangere is not Massignac's daughter. She is the illegitimate daughter of Dorgeroux and Massignac's wife (born five months after Massignac's marriage). Her "suspicious behavior" stems entirely from fear: Velmot threatened to kill Victorien if she did not remain silent, forcing her into a passive role on the periphery of the conspiracy. She never stopped loving Victorien, and at a critical moment her actions saved Massignac's life — thereby preserving the formula.

Reasoning Path Reconstruction

First Reasoning Path: The Mystery of the Venus Projection

At approximately 10% (Chapter 2), an attentive reader notices that the wall's base has been rebuilt into an "inclined plane" and that Dorgeroux plans to build a second theater on the other side of the garden. But the detail is too subtle to assign meaning at this point.

At approximately 42% (Chapter 8), Dorgeroux scratches "B-ray… B E R G E…" before dying. A linguistic code is embedded here: if the reader interprets this in French, "Berger" means "shepherd," and the "Shepherd's Star" (l'Etoile du Berger) is the common French name for Venus. But this requires the reader to possess knowledge of French astronomical folk terminology and to have resisted the "Bergeronnette" misreading.

At approximately 57% (Chapter 12), the "Shapes" images show three-handed, three-eyed non-human beings moving through a vertical city — a direct depiction of Venusian life. In the same chapter, the images disappear as clouds pass over. These two clues appear together and form the clearest "scientific clue combination" in the entire book. A reader who can connect "the three-eyed trademark of the triangular beings," "the cloud interference," and "the westward-facing inclined wall" now stands at the threshold of the puzzle.

At approximately 65% (Chapter 13), Prevotelle's telephone call asks whether the reverse face of the wall has the same inclination; Victorien confirms it does. At this point, the reasoning path is fully formed: Venus (morning/evening star, alternating east and west) → gravitational rays → special coating → images.

Second Reasoning Path: The Identity of the Killer

At approximately 35% (Chapter 7), Victorien fights the killer hand-to-hand in the forest; the man's "ferocious eyes" leave a deep impression. In the same chapter, the wall image in the abandoned church presents the same ferocity in the three-eyes preview (this is the Venusian mechanism of "previewing the protagonist's eye expression").

At approximately 47% (Chapter 9), Massignac attacks Victorien in the hotel. During the struggle, Victorien covers Massignac's lower face and sees only the eyes — "Those are the eyes! The eyes on the church wall, the eyes of the man who pinned me down in the forest!" This is the unmistakable "eye-recognition moment." A careful reader now has sufficient evidence to conclude: Massignac is both the killer and Velmot's co-conspirator.

At approximately 90% (Chapter 18), the wall image directly projects the sequence of Dorgeroux's murder. All suspicion becomes confirmation.

Third Reasoning Path: Berangere's True Role

Berangere's psychological portrait runs through the entire book in constant contradiction — she appears to know Velmot but lies about it; she appears in the garden at night; she reappears at the showing and embraces Victorien. The only positive clue the author provides to counter her apparent guilt is in Chapter 5 (28%): the wall image reveals her secretly smiling with genuine love at the fallen Victorien. This detail hints that she is not a cold-hearted conspirator. But her true origins (as Dorgeroux's daughter) constitute a "hidden fact" not revealed until Massignac's deathbed confession (95%); before that point, the reader can almost never deduce it from the textual logic alone.

Supplementary Notes

The Anomalous Place of The Three Eyes in the History of Detective Fiction

This work is a paradox. In Chapter 8, Leblanc has the narrator explicitly declare: "This is neither a detective novel nor a love story" — an extraordinarily rare authorial proclamation in which the author strips away the detective fiction label from his own work. Yet structurally it contains a complete criminal narrative (murder, theft, conspiracy), a clue system (hints, misdirection, eye identification), and a final revelation. It disowns the detective novel label because its central mystery is not "what did a person do" but "what is the universe doing."

The Nested Structure of Three Puzzles

The Three Eyes presents an uncommonly nested puzzle design: the scientific mystery (Venus projection) → the criminal mystery (the killer's identity) → the emotional mystery (Berangere's true heart). The clues for all three share a single core device — the "three-eyes image" is simultaneously the phenomenon of the scientific mystery itself, the clue system of the criminal mystery (the Venusians faithfully record and preview the good and evil of human affairs), and in Chapter 5 the revealer of Berangere's emotional truth. No other novel uses a single "mysterious phenomenon" to serve three fundamentally different kinds of mystery at once.

The Rare Fusion of Leblanc's Science-Fiction Vision and Detective Technique

Leblanc is best known for Arsene Lupin, the courteous gentleman-thief turned amateur detective. The Three Eyes, however, is his most ambitious "non-Lupin" work, attempting to pack a complete Venusian cosmology inside the shell of a detective novel. The resulting difficulty is strikingly uneven: the clues to the criminal portion (eye identification) fall within the range of traditional detective reasoning (58% convergence); the core answer to the scientific portion (Venus projection), though supported by clues (clouds, the inclined wall, the dying clue), requires specific knowledge to deduce independently before Prevotelle's report (68% convergence); and Berangere's origins amount almost entirely to an authorial gift rather than a reader's achievement (95%).

"The Dual Reading of the Dying Clue" as the Book's Most Elegant Puzzle Design

"B-ray… B E R G E…" is the most ingeniously designed puzzle nucleus in the book. It is simultaneously:

  1. The scientific answer: B-ray of gravitation + the Shepherd's Star (l'Etoile du Berger = Venus)
  2. The greatest misdirection: "BERGE" = "Bergeronnette" = Berangere, casting a fog of suspicion over her that shrouds the second half of the book

This "bilingual double meaning" cipher design — exploiting the peculiarity of "Berger" as a French astronomical colloquialism — represents the closest Leblanc comes in this book to the pure puzzle craftsmanship of Father Brown or Poirot. Even buried beneath layers of science-fiction philosophy, it stands out as a piece of quietly exceptional craft.

Leblanc's Interplanetary Ethics

Prevotelle's report reveals that the images the Venusians display are not random: they show humanity's most important love (the history of kissing), its most important conflicts (revolutions and wars), its most important suffering (the Passion of Christ), and its most important demonstrations of good and evil (various murders) — ending finally with the death of Dorgeroux as the "last entry in the sequence." This carefully curated "thematic exhibition" implies that the Venusians are not merely observing but "judging" — choosing what is worth transmitting. Through this, Leblanc elevates the detective puzzle into a metaphysical question: the universe is watching us — are we worthy of being seen?
