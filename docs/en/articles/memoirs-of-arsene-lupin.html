Memoirs of Arsène Lupin — Deducibility Analysis

Basic Information

  • Author: Maurice Leblanc
  • Year: 1925
  • Type: Novel
  • Detective: Arsène Lupin (appearing under his young identity as "Raoul de Limezy")
  • Core Mystery Type: whodunit + howdunit + unreliable source within narrative

Overall Ratings

Indicator Rating Notes
Deducibility ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Average convergence point 79% — Joséphine's identity mystery converges at 60%, the treasure location mystery at 85%, murder confirmation at 92%; average of the three: 79%
Fog Rating ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Joséphine functions as an unreliable information source embedded within the narrative itself, actively manipulating the reader through multiple layers of deception: the "immortality legend," the "another woman" alibi, and the fabricated shipwreck death; the narrative structure itself becomes a tool of misdirection

Synopsis

In the late nineteenth century, the young Raoul de Limezy — who will later become Arsène Lupin — intercepts a secret letter aboard a train bound for Normandy. The letter, written by a fanatical Catholic named Beaumagnan to his co-conspirators, outlines a plan to kidnap and eliminate a mysterious woman. That woman is Joséphine Balsamo: she claims — or is rumored — to be the reincarnation of the Countess of Cagliostro, an immortal woman who has lived for centuries.

While falling deeply in love with Joséphine, Raoul is drawn into an ancient treasure hunt spanning Normandy. At the heart of the puzzle is a Latin inscription — Ad lapidem currebat olim regina ("The queen once ran toward that stone") — hidden across seven ancient abbeys in Normandy, pointing toward an immense medieval treasure. Three factions pursue different ends across the Norman countryside: Beaumagnan's secret religious sect, Joséphine and her associates, and Raoul himself, locked in a contest of scheming, betrayal, and love.

Three mystery threads run from the first chapter to the last, only surfacing in the final chapters: Joséphine's true age, whether she is truly a killer, and where the treasure is hidden. Has Raoul fallen in love with a genius con artist — or a cold-blooded serial murderer?

Truth Breakdown

Truth One: Joséphine's True Identity — Is She Really Ageless?

  • Clue Convergence Point: approximately 60%
  • Answer: Joséphine is not truly immortal. She is the daughter — or a descendant — of the original "Countess of Cagliostro," and she sustains the myth of the "eternal woman" through forged documents, inherited techniques of deception, and a striking physical resemblance to Renaissance-era portraits. Her "agelessness" is an elaborate identity fraud, not a supernatural phenomenon.

Truth Two: The Location of the Treasure — The ALCOR Mystery

  • Clue Convergence Point: approximately 85%
  • Answer: The initial letters of the Latin inscription Ad lapidem currebat olim regina ("The queen once ran toward that stone") spell A-L-C-O-R — Alcor, the companion star beside Mizar in the Big Dipper of Ursa Major. The seven ancient abbeys of the Pays de Caux (the Norman plateau) correspond geographically to the shape of the Big Dipper; the position corresponding to Alcor falls at Mesnil-sous-Jumièges, in the vicinity of the estate of Agnès Sorel — and the treasure is buried beneath a certain stone there.

Key Clues

# Clue Approximate Position Notes
1 The full Latin inscription appears for the first time, scattered across stone carvings or documents in Normandy's abbeys ~10–25% The raw material of the puzzle; first-time readers are unlikely to immediately register the acronym connection
2 The geographic locations of the seven abbeys accumulate gradually over the course of the investigation ~30–60% Individual abbeys do not form a pattern; all seven points are required to compare against a star map
3 Once Raoul has all seven abbey locations, he plots them on a map and discovers their arrangement matches the Big Dipper ~80% The star-map correspondence is the pivotal step in solving the puzzle; at this moment, a reader thinking in parallel can also derive "ALCOR"
4 "ALCOR" as an acronym is explicitly identified, confirmed as pointing to the companion star Alcor in Ursa Major ~85% Convergence point — the final critical piece; the precise location of the treasure is now fully revealed
5 The connection between Mesnil-sous-Jumièges and Agnès Sorel is disclosed, confirming the exact burial site ~87% Translates the stellar correspondence into a specific location on the ground

Supporting Clues

# Clue Approximate Position Notes
1 Beaumagnan's secret sect has tracked this puzzle for a long time and holds partial clues ~3% Confirms the puzzle's existence and historical depth — if even fanatical seekers have pursued it for decades, it must have genuine substance
2 Joséphine is clearly pursuing the same answer and possesses certain clues Throughout The three-way contest confirms the puzzle's scarcity and value, and obliquely signals that the answer is not obvious
3 The historical religious context of Normandy (the medieval abbey network) provides a real-world anchor for the puzzle Throughout Makes the conceit of "seven abbeys forming a geometric figure" plausible within a historical framework
4 Latin was the universal language of medieval ecclesiastical settings — a Latin inscription is natural historical camouflage Throughout Reduces readers' instinctive alertness that "this is a cipher," making the acronym riddle look, on the surface, like an ordinary religious motto

False Clues

# Clue Approximate Position Misdirection
1 The individual stone carvings, legends, and local historical details at each abbey ~20–55% Readers are easily drawn into treating each abbey as the answer in itself, rather than considering the geometric relationship among the group of abbeys as a whole
2 The literal reading of "the queen once ran toward that stone" ~10% Leads readers to search historical records for "which queen, which stone," rather than recognizing this as an acronym cipher
3 Beaumagnan's faction repeatedly heading in wrong directions (they have pursued this for decades without success) ~3–70% If such obsessive seekers have gone astray, readers readily conclude the puzzle is far more complex than it actually is

Truth Three: Is Joséphine Truly a Murderer?

  • Clue Convergence Point: approximately 92%
  • Answer: Yes. Joséphine bears direct or indirect responsibility for multiple deaths. Madame Rousselin's hand is brutally mutilated (carried out by Joséphine's associates); she orders the killing of Clarisse (though this order is secretly sabotaged by Dominique, and Clarisse actually survives). Joséphine is never convicted in any legal sense, but her responses and behavioral patterns throughout the novel lead to a moral confirmation at the end that she treats human life as a tool.

Reconstruction of the Reasoning Paths

First Reasoning Path: Joséphine's True Age

This path is relatively accessible to careful readers. At the 15% mark, Prince Arcola's recognition superficially supports the "immortality legend," but simultaneously plants the seed of an alternative reading: what the Prince witnessed was "a striking resemblance," not confirmed identity. By the portrait passage at 30%, a reader consciously resisting the romantic atmosphere should be able to notice that there is a logical leap between "resemblance" and "the same person."

The true reasoning convergence occurs at 60%, when Raoul explicitly proposes the "mother-daughter transmission of identity" hypothesis in Chapter Seven, using it to account for all the seemingly supernatural phenomena encountered so far. This is the text extending a hand to the reader and saying: "You may now understand her through a rational framework."

Second Reasoning Path: The Location of the Treasure

This is the most complex puzzle in the novel, requiring readers to simultaneously track three streams of information: the initial letters of the Latin inscription, the geographic positions of the seven abbeys, and knowledge of the constellations.

Between roughly 30% and 60%, the seven abbeys appear one by one; but if readers have not grasped the dimension of "geometric relationships on a map," these locations register only as settings, not as clues. At 80%, Raoul marks all positions on a map and discovers the match with the star chart — readers who have kept pace with Raoul's reasoning at this moment have their first real opportunity to grasp the significance of "ALCOR" simultaneously with him. This is a rare instance of a puzzle designed for "reasoning in lockstep with the protagonist": the solution does not surface before the protagonist reaches it, and the reader's convergence point is almost identical to the protagonist's own.

Third Reasoning Path: Joséphine's Nature as a Murderer

This is the most convoluted path and carries the greatest emotional tension of any mystery thread in the novel.

At 50%, the "another woman" alibi successfully suspends readers' judgment, because Joséphine's love for Raoul genuinely appears real; readers and Raoul together fall into the moral dilemma of "can love coexist with evil?" The faked death at 80% causes readers to lose their narrative focus for continuing to pursue her culpability. Only at 88–90%, when Clarisse's kill order and the confrontation surface, does every prior act of violence get re-examined in a new light. Raoul's verdict at 92% is the reader's final confirmation node.

What makes this path distinctive is that it demands readers resist the narrator's own bias. Raoul loves Joséphine deeply and therefore argues for her defense repeatedly, granting her "the benefit of the doubt" again and again. Readers must assess Joséphine more swiftly and more coolly than Raoul can. Technically, this is a subtler challenge than the standard unreliable narrator: the narrator himself is reliable, but his judgment is distorted by emotion.

Additional Notes

The Special Place of Memoirs of Arsène Lupin in the Lupin Series

This work occupies a singular position in Leblanc's Lupin series: it is an origin story, tracing the adolescence of Raoul de Limezy before he became "Arsène Lupin." Joséphine Balsamo, as the first great and devastating love of his life, shapes in some meaningful sense the mature Lupin's entire attitude toward the law, toward women, and toward the "perfect crime."

Seen from this angle, the book is not a puzzle about "who did it" but a character-formation narrative about "how Lupin became Lupin" — the mystery elements serve the portraiture of character, rather than the reverse.

Core of the puzzle Deductive reasoning Historical cipher and geographic decryption
Narrative perspective An observing assistant (Watson) The protagonist himself (emotionally invested)
Transparency of clues Relatively fair and open Actively contaminated by the information source (Joséphine)
Role of the reader Chasing the detective Trying to surpass a narrator blinded by love

Joséphine as a Narrative Weapon

What makes Joséphine distinctive is that she is not merely the answer to the puzzle — she is simultaneously its active architect. She systematically feeds false information to Raoul (and to the reader): "I am a woman who has not aged in centuries"; "That wasn't me, it was another woman"; "I am dead" — each deception appearing at precisely the moment when readers are most vulnerable, most willing to believe her. This design — in which someone inside the puzzle is dynamically manipulating the puzzle's information — gives Memoirs of Arsène Lupin a narrative complexity far exceeding that of its contemporaries.

The Historical Foundations of the ALCOR Puzzle

Alcor (also known as 80 Ursae Majoris) is the companion star beside Mizar in the Big Dipper of Ursa Major. In antiquity it was commonly used as a vision test — whether one could distinguish Alcor beside Mizar determined whether one possessed soldier-grade eyesight. Leblanc's choice of this "hidden star" as the answer is itself an elegant double meaning: Alcor is the star visible only to those with sufficient powers of observation, and the solution too is visible only to readers with sufficient powers of observation.

A Note for Readers Unfamiliar with European History

"Count Cagliostro" was a real historical figure in Europe: an eighteenth-century Italian adventurer and impostor who was active at the French court and entangled in the political scandals of the age of Marie Antoinette. By borrowing this backdrop, Leblanc gives the legend of "the Countess of Cagliostro" genuine historical weight. For European readers of Leblanc's day, that surname was already a byword for mystery, deception, and near-magical personal magnetism — no further explanation required.
