Arsène Lupin, Super-Sleuth — Deducibility Analysis

Basic Information

  • Author: Maurice Leblanc
  • Year: 1927 (English translation; French original L'Agence Barnett et Cie, 1923)
  • Type: Novel
  • Detective: Arsène Lupin (alias Ralph de Limézy / Baron de Limézy)
  • Core mystery type: whodunit + identity mystery + treasure hunt

Deducibility Ratings

Metric Stars Notes
Deducibility ⭐⭐⭐ Average convergence point ~69% — key clues in the train murder case fully emerge around 70%, Lupin's identity can be inferred at ~35%, and the two major secrets converge at ~85%
Fog Rating ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Highly misleading: the entire novel is narrated from the perspective of an "obvious suspect," and the narrator's viewpoint encourages readers to misidentify the victim as the killer

Synopsis

In late April Paris, the adventurer "Baron Ralph" (Ralph de Limézy) casually notices two women on a boulevard: an English woman named Constance Bakersfield and a green-eyed French woman (later revealed as Aurelie d'Asteux). Their fates become entangled with his from that moment on.

That same evening, he boards the Marseilles express heading south, sharing a carriage with Miss Bakersfield. In the dead of night, three masked assailants suddenly appear. They knock Ralph unconscious and bind him, strangle the English woman, then murder two men in the rear carriage (the Loubeaux brothers). As the attackers flee, one of the assailant's masks slips — revealing the green-eyed French woman Ralph had seen that afternoon at a confectionery.

Ralph cleverly escapes the scene and, throughout the journey, shields this woman he calls the "green-eyed girl" from Detective Marescal's pursuit — never understanding whether she is a murderer or an innocent victim.

The chase unfolds. Ralph traverses Nice (a villa burglary), Monaco (a blackmail trap), Toulouse (a theatre disguise), a Pyrenean convent, and a Parisian townhouse — at each stop enemies await, and each crisis is resolved at the last possible moment. In the end, the truth is revealed: the reality of the train murders completely overturns all appearances, and the childhood secret that Aurelie has been guarding points to a submerged Roman city — the Fountain of Youth hidden beneath a mountain lake.

Truth Analysis

The novel contains four overlapping mysteries, each with different clue convergence points:

Mystery One: The Express Train Murder — Who Is the Killer? (Core Whodunit)

  • Clue convergence point: ~70%
  • Answer: The true masterminds of the attack were Jodot and the Loubeaux brothers. The three crept from the fourth carriage into the fifth in disguise, mistakenly killing Miss Bakersfield (whom they took for Aurelie), then moved to the rear carriage intending to seize William and Aurelie. William shot and killed the Loubeaux brothers with his revolver, and Jodot fled. William and Aurelie then donned the bloodstained clothes of the dead and escaped — which is why the police completely reversed the identities of the fleeing "killers" and the supposedly dead "victims."

Key Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Notes
1 Aurelie, on the night of her arrest, flatly declared "I did not kill anyone!" Chapter 3 / ~15% She said it with absolute conviction, not hedging; however, in the narrative context at that moment, it is easily overlooked by readers
2 The person who purchased the revolver was "a tall, slender man whose female companion called him William" Chapter 5 / ~57% The revolver is traced to William (i.e., William Ancivel), directly implying that William was the one who fired it
3 In the Loubeaux brothers' villa, Ralph discovers a piece of black cloth with three circular holes cut out of it — the material used to make the masks Chapter 8 / ~70% Decisive clue: if the Loubeaux brothers were victims, why does their home contain the mask material left at the crime scene? This clue establishes that they were the planners, not victims
4 Jodot had been a business partner of the Loubeaux brothers (the firm "Loubeaux Frères et Jodot") Chapter 8 / ~73% The three were originally partners, making it easy to understand that the three conspired together

Supporting Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Notes
1 In Chapter 2, Marescal puts forward the hypothesis that "the victims were actually the targets": the assailants had expected to find two male passengers, but stumbled upon a man and a woman and fled in panic Chapter 2 / ~12% Marescal's reasoning is partially correct; he only misidentified who "the real target" was — it was Aurelie, not the Loubeaux brothers
2 The green-eyed woman (Aurelie), when arrested, was wearing bloodstained clothes and a mask, yet throughout she wept and said "I want to see a judge" Chapter 3 / ~18% A genuine killer is unlikely to weep and demand to confess to a judge; her reaction resembles that of a terrified innocent person
3 Jodot ambushes Aurelie in the garden of the villa and says "I told you, on the express…" Chapter 4 / ~40% Jodot has direct memory of the train incident and his tone is "you owe me an explanation," indicating that he was the true planner
4 Aurelie says she "saw Jodot's face in the carriage" and was subsequently silenced by his threats Chapter 11 / ~92% Her silence was due to Jodot threatening to expose her stepfather Bregeac's past crimes — this clue explains her motivation for staying silent throughout the novel

False Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Misleading direction
1 Aurelie appears in the corridor of the crime carriage wearing bloodstained clothes and a mask End of Chapter 1 / ~8% Readers (and Ralph) immediately assume she is the killer; in reality she was wearing the Loubeaux brothers' bloodstained clothes as a disguise
2 When Ralph was knocked unconscious, the "attackers" numbered three — one man and one woman — and both Marescal and Ralph assume the woman was Aurelie Chapter 2 / ~12% Readers naturally follow the assumption that the three criminals = Jodot + William + Aurelie; the correct answer is Jodot + the two Loubeaux brothers
3 Jodot repeatedly appears "chasing Aurelie through Nice and Monaco demanding she reveal the secret," casting him as the typical villain Throughout This conceals the fact that Jodot was actually the mastermind of the train murder — his real threat was "if you speak out, I'll expose your stepfather"

Mystery Two: The Identity of Arsène Lupin (Identity Mystery)

  • Clue convergence point: ~35%
  • Answer: Ralph de Limézy is Arsène Lupin, operating under an alias (as hinted in Chapter 1 by Miss Bakersfield).

Key Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Notes
1 Miss Bakersfield on the train notes that the monogram on Ralph's hat reads "H.V.," associating it with "Horace Valmont" — one of Lupin's aliases Chapter 1 / ~5% The author hints at his identity directly through the sharpest observer in the scene (Bakersfield)
2 The name "Raoul de Limézy" is very similar to "Ralph d'Andrésy" (another of Lupin's aliases) Chapter 1 / ~5% Bakersfield explicitly notes the structural similarity of the two names, and that both fit Lupin's habitual alias pattern
3 The abilities Ralph demonstrates — picking locks with ease, unparalleled disguise skills, physical agility, and psychological manipulation — all match Lupin's signature traits Chapters 1–5 / ~5–55% The character's abilities are themselves clues: an ordinary "adventurer" could not possess these skills
4 Marescal compares Lupin's old case against Ganimard, saying "tonight's methods demonstrated the same degree of control," yet assumes the man in question is not present Chapter 3 / ~18% The author deliberately has Marescal praise "Lupin's style" in front of Ralph, creating dramatic irony — the subject of conversation is the very person being spoken to

Supporting Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Notes
1 Upon learning of Miss Bakersfield's dying words, Ralph proactively takes the red leather wallet, saying "she entrusted the documents to me" Chapter 2 / ~12% Lupin's characteristic moral ambiguity: stealing documents under the pretext of protection; readers familiar with the Lupin series can recognise this pattern
2 Ralph uses the phrase "Could you oblige me with a light?" as a signature line in front of Marescal; it is cited multiple times throughout the novel Throughout In the Lupin series, "a light" is Lupin's habitual taunt when operating under an alias; veteran readers see through it immediately

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Misleading direction
1 Chapter 2 explicitly states "we will continue to call him Ralph, for that is his name in this part of the story" Chapter 2 / ~10% On the surface this says "we're using this name for now," but it is in fact the author deliberately leaving the true identity unresolved

Mystery Three: Bregeac's Past Crime (Past Crime)

  • Clue convergence point: ~85%
  • Answer: About eighteen years earlier, Bregeac betrayed Aurelie's grandfather Etienne d'Asteux (the keeper of the Fountain of Youth secret) to Jodot and the Loubeaux brothers, in exchange for the three men's freedom. They broke into the old man's sickbed in the dead of night demanding the secret, and ultimately tortured him to death. Bregeac was afterwards blackmailed by William's mother, forced to sign a written confession, which was sealed in a bottle and became the object of competition among all parties.

Key Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Notes
1 Jodot repeatedly loiters outside the Loubeaux brothers' old house and has a child search the cellar and vacant land Chapter 8 / ~73% He is searching for the bottle containing the confession — indicating that incriminating evidence against him is at large
2 Bregeac is also covertly monitoring Jodot's movements Chapter 8 / ~76% The mutually threatening relationship between the two implies a past conspiracy; an innocent man would have no need to be so nervous
3 The document in the bottle: "This accusation is true. The following is my formal confession. I alone take responsibility for the crimes committed; Jodot and Loubeaux bear no fault. — Bregeac" Chapter 8 / ~87% A direct written confession confirming Bregeac's crime; but "Jodot is innocent" is a lie — it is the letter of indemnity that Bregeac was forced to sign
4 In Chapter 10, Ralph fully reconstructs the old case: Bregeac knew Jodot and the Loubeaux brothers (acquaintances from Cherbourg), used his official position to release them from prison, in exchange for their eliminating Aurelie's grandfather on his behalf Chapter 10 / ~90% A complete reconstruction after integrating all clues

False Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Misleading direction
1 Bregeac repeatedly demonstrates his "love" for Aurelie, leading readers to assume he is merely a man excessively attached to his stepdaughter Chapters 8–9 / ~70–90% This conceals his true motive: he had approached and persecuted Aurelie in order to seize the secret

Mystery Four: Aurelie's Secret — The Fountain of Youth (The Secret)

  • Clue convergence point: ~90%
  • Answer: A submerged ancient Roman city called "Juvains," and beneath it a mineral spring of high radioactivity (the Fountain of Youth, Eau de Jouvence). At the end of the fourth century AD, the last Roman governor of Gaul, Fabius Aralla, ordered the sluice gates closed to flood the entire city in order to protect the temple and the spring from barbarian invaders. Fifteen hundred years later, Aurelie's grandfather and his close friend the Marquis de Talencay restored the sluice mechanism and secretly guarded this underwater wonder.

Key Clues

# Clue Location (chapter/percentage) Notes
1 The bottle's label: "EAU DE JOUVENCE" (Water of Youth) and a chemical composition analysis Chapter 8 / ~87% "Jouvence" (youth) is the linguistic key to solving the mystery
2 Ralph finds that the composition almost exactly replicates the analysis of the Royat thermal spring in the Auvergne region, and locates the village of "Juvains" on a map Chapter 12 / ~90% Juvains = Latin Juventia = French Jouvence = youth; the place name is the answer
3 Aurelie visited that place at age six; she remembers church bells, a peculiar colour to the water, and strangely shaped rocks; her mother told her she could only reveal it to "the person you fall in love with" Chapter 6 / ~35% The outline of the secret, but no specific location — readers cannot yet pinpoint it
4 The Marquis de Talencay's documents describe the sluice mechanism and a plan of the underwater city Chapters 13–14 / ~93% The complete revelation of the secret

Reasoning Path Reconstruction

Main Mystery: The Train Murder

The "obvious truth" from the reader's perspective is that the green-eyed woman (Aurelie) and William are the assailants and that she personally killed the Loubeaux brothers. Yet attentive readers should notice, as early as Chapter 2 (~12%), Marescal's first inference — that the assailants originally sought "two male passengers" but blundered into an attack on Miss Bakersfield, implying that the attackers did not know Bakersfield, did not know Aurelie, and certainly were not William himself (who was present in person and had no need to disguise himself).

At ~57%, the revolver is traced to William; if William were a victim, why would the victim's personal weapon become the murder weapon? This contradiction should alert the reader.

The decisive node comes at 70%: Ralph finds the mask-making cloth in the Loubeaux household. At that point, one can reasonably deduce: 1. The Loubeaux brothers possessed mask material → they were the attackers, not the victims 2. William killed with his own gun → he acted in self-defence, not murder 3. Aurelie fled wearing the victims' bloodstained clothes → she was in disguise, not the killer

The complete revelation at 90% merely formally announces what the reader could already have deduced.

Identity Mystery: Who Is Ralph?

Chapter 1 (~5%) — Miss Bakersfield's "H.V. = Horace Valmont = an alias of Lupin" hint is virtually an explicit statement. Every subsequent action Ralph takes echoes the characteristic traits of the Lupin series. Readers who are familiar with Leblanc will likely be certain by 20%; general readers, after comparing character descriptions, can confirm by approximately 60%.

Additional Notes

The Inverted Structure of the "Detective Novel"

The fundamental fascination of Arsène Lupin, Super-Sleuth lies in a thorough structural reversal: "the victim is the killer, and the fugitive is the innocent." This is one of Leblanc's most masterful narrative designs — readers and the policeman in the book (Marescal) are placed in the same position, deceived by carefully constructed "visual evidence" (bloodstained clothes, mask, flight).

The novel spends approximately 70% of its length allowing this misapprehension to "ferment," until the black cloth at the Loubeaux house in Chapter 8 provides the physical basis for the reversal, and Chapter 10 formally announces the full truth. This delay does not arise because the author "hid the clues" — rather, the key to the solution is buried within the narrative flow, so that any reader who remains alert can reconstruct it independently — but most will be carried along by "common sense."

Leblanc's Narrative Strategy: Wrapping the Puzzle in Adventure

Unlike Doyle's or Christie's classical deductive approach, Leblanc's puzzles are never "clean" — every clue is wrapped in the trappings of romantic adventure (escapes, kisses, car chases, a city drowned by flood). Readers are typically drawn in by the heroic surface narrative and overlook the deeper logical structure. This is the fundamental reason for the higher Fog Rating: it is not the quantity of false clues, but rather the entire narrative context itself that functions as a red herring.

"A Light" and the Aesthetics of Irony

"Could you oblige me with a light?" appears no fewer than five times in the novel, always just before or after Marescal is outwitted by Lupin. This seemingly innocuous request is a kind of self-declaration by Lupin — it marks the moment "this man's elegant mockery has once again succeeded." Its final appearance comes in the epilogue after the novel's conclusion: Lupin, now operating under the alias Lenormand (police chief), once again makes Marescal shudder with "a light." Leblanc uses this symbol to compress the entire detective theme of the novel into an eternal joke: the clever man is always standing right next to you before you even know it.

Comparison with Other Works in the Lupin Series

Within the series, this novel belongs to the mature period's "long-form adventure-mystery hybrid." Compared to the early short story collections (such as Arsène Lupin, Gentleman-Burglar), which employed single-mystery structures, this novel overlays four mysteries, features a background mystery spanning eighteen years, and runs three parallel threads — romantic, suspenseful, and historical — representing the most ambitious scope of Leblanc's storytelling. The revelation of the submerged city of Juvains (Chapter 14) resembles a Vernesian fantastical exploration more than the conclusion of a conventional crime novel, and this cross-genre blending is the defining hallmark of Leblanc's style.
