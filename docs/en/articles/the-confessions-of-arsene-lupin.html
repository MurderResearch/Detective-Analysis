The Confessions of Arsène Lupin — Deducibility Analysis

Basic Information

  • Author: Maurice Leblanc
  • Year: 1913
  • Type: Short story collection (10 stories)
  • Detective: Arsène Lupin — part-time gentleman thief and amateur detective
  • Core Mystery Types: whodunit / howdunit / hiding-place puzzle / identity deception

Deducibility Rating

Indicator Stars Notes
Deducibility ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Average convergence point approximately 78% — key clues in each story only come together in the latter portions; readers require exceptionally strong memory and patience; some stories are nearly impossible to solve in advance
Fog Rating ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Leblanc habitually uses Lupin as a first-person information controller who actively conceals his reasoning process; the truth is typically revealed only at the last moment in a "magician's reveal" style, keeping readers in a state of prolonged information asymmetry

Synopsis

The Confessions of Arsène Lupin is a collection of 10 short stories that Leblanc compiled for the Lupin series, recorded by a hypothetical "narrator" who transcribes the adventures Lupin recounts in his own words. The book presents a distinctive image of Lupin — he is simultaneously thief and detective; he both commits crimes and solves them; at times he assumes the role of a knight rescuing women in distress, at other times he employs trickery to fleece hypocrites.

The ten stories in brief: - Story 1 "Two Hundred Thousand Francs Reward!": Beginning with a coded signal, it unravels a case of murder combined with fraud, with the true culprit hidden within the person offering the reward. - Story 2 "The Wedding Ring": Lupin (under the alias Horace Velmont) rescues a countess being persecuted by her husband, substituting her ring through sleight of hand. - Story 3 "The Sign of the Shadow": Solving a century-old family mystery, with 18 diamonds hidden in the crack of a sundial. - Story 4 "Lupin Trapped": Lupin is himself captured by a widow's scheme, then mysteriously rescued by the widow's nephew (actually a niece in disguise). - Story 5 "The Red Silk Scarf": Lupin reconstructs an entire murder from a bundle of discards thrown into the Seine. - Story 6 "The Shadow of Death": Lupin uncovers the mastermind — a stepfather feigning illness who is attempting to murder his stepdaughter. - Story 7 "Shadowy Love": A tribute to Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue," with an ape as the killer. - Story 8 "Lupin's Marriage": Lupin impersonates a noble cousin, forcing a duke to give his daughter in marriage to him, yet the conclusion is unexpectedly filled with romantic pathos. - Story 9 "The Invisible Prisoner": An elderly vagrant disguises himself as a scarecrow and hides for a month; Lupin solves the puzzle with "three stalks of wheat." - Story 10 "Edith Swan-Neck": The grandest scheme of all — Colonel Sparmiento himself is Lupin, and even his death is fabricated.

This analysis focuses on the four stories with the highest deductive content: Stories 1, 3, 9, and 10.

In-Depth Analysis of Selected Stories

Story 1: "Two Hundred Thousand Francs Reward!"

Mystery Type: whodunit + howdunit (Who killed Lavernoux? Where is the Baroness? What is the safe's combination?)

Convergence Points by Aspect: - Discovery of the code "ETNA": approximately 12% (deliberately misspelled letters) - Identity of the true culprit (Baron Repstein): approximately 72% (when Lupin directly accuses the Baron in his study) - The Baroness already dead, body in the safe: approximately 90% (the moment Lupin opens the safe) - Average convergence point: 58% → ⭐⭐⭐ (moderate difficulty, but "the Baroness is in the safe" is almost impossible to deduce in advance)

Truth 1: The Coded Signal ETNA and the Safe Combination

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 12%
  • Answer: The message Lavernoux sent via sunlight signals contained deliberately misspelled English words — "necessery" (an extra e), "atack" (missing a t), "ennemy" (an extra n), "prudance" (a replacing e) — the first incorrect letter of each misspelled word spells ETNA, the name of the Baron's racehorse, which is also the safe's combination.
Key Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 Abnormal spelling errors appear in the message: "necessery," "atack," "ennemy," "prudance" ~6% Lavernoux, as the Baron's private secretary, could not possibly make such elementary errors; the mistakes are deliberately crafted as a code
2 Lupin immediately notices these errors and falls into thought ~8% The narrator explicitly states "his expression changed, that little furrowing of the brow appeared" — signaling to the reader that this is a crucial discovery
3 The Baron owns the celebrated racehorse ETNA in the racing world, winner of the current year's Derby and Grand Prix ~10% (background information) ETNA = the key letters from the four misspellings, and also the symbol of the Baron's most prized asset
Supporting Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 Lavernoux uses a pocket mirror to create flashing signals, clearly transmitting an urgent message to someone outside the window ~4% Indicates he is being confined and must seek help through covert means
2 His English friend Hargrove rushes to the police station immediately upon receiving the signal ~16% Indicates the message contained more than what Lupin intercepted; the unfinished sentence held additional information
Red Herrings
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Misleading Direction
1 Lavernoux's identity as a "doctor" ~22% Leads readers to assume Lavernoux is merely a patient, rather than a hostage being forcibly held
2 The Baron offering 200,000 francs to find his missing wife, presenting himself as a victim Throughout the first half Casts the Baron as a wronged husband betrayed by his wife, completely concealing the possibility that he is the mastermind

Truth 2: The True Culprit and the Whereabouts of the Baroness

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 88%
  • Answer: Baron Repstein disguised himself as a doctor to confine Lavernoux, then killed him with a tiepin. He had already murdered his wife, concealed her body in the safe, and arranged for an accomplice woman to impersonate the Baroness in flight, leading police on a pan-European chase to cover his escape with the money.
Key Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 The time the "old doctor" went out corresponds precisely to Lavernoux's time of death ~20% The doctor does not appear to be a real doctor: he had only left twenty minutes before Lavernoux was found dead
2 The wound is as fine as a pinhole, not a knife wound — "a very long needle piercing the heart" ~22% Such an unusual weapon points toward a specific individual
3 The Baron's tiepin: exceptionally long, triangular in cross-section, shaped like an elegant dagger, with a pearl of considerable value ~60% Lupin notices how far the pin protrudes from the waistcoat and judges it to be that "needle"
4 The Baron admits the chain of facts to Lupin in his study, then draws a pistol in retaliation; his behavior confirms the accusation ~75% A truly innocent person would not draw a gun at such a moment
5 The safe is opened and the Baroness's body is revealed ~88% The final and most shocking confirmation: all mysteries converge here
Supporting Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 The Baron sells his racetrack, Paris townhouse, and villa, preparing to flee ~12% Only a person preparing to flee needs to liquidate all assets
2 The Baron "looks like an honest man" — Lupin himself says "you have the face of an honest man" ~75% Leblanc uses Lupin's words to remind the reader: appearance is the finest disguise
Red Herrings
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Misleading Direction
1 The "Baroness" arrested in Belgium ~10% Leads readers to believe the real Baroness is fleeing across Europe, making her pursuit seem like the main thread
2 The Baron's "sorrow" and "moist eyes" ~62% His emotional performance causes Lupin (and the reader) to momentarily doubt — is he really the villain?

Story 3: "The Sign of the Shadow"

Mystery Type: Treasure-hiding puzzle (Where is it?)

Convergence Points by Aspect: - That "2" represents "2 o'clock" rather than "Year II of the Republican Calendar": approximately 90% (when Lupin reveals it) - The diamonds hidden in the sundial crack: approximately 95% - Average convergence point: 92% → ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (extreme difficulty: nearly impossible to solve independently before the revelation)

Truth: 18 Diamonds in the Sundial Crack

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 92%
  • Answer: The "2" in the date "15.4.2" represents "2 o'clock in the afternoon," not Year II of the Republican Calendar. Every year on April 15 at 2 p.m., the sundial's gnomon shadow falls precisely on a crack in the marble surface. The tax collector d'Ernemont, before being arrested, was in the garden and at that moment filled the crack with 18 diamonds. The madman Charles's "pilgrimage" also ends at 5:27 p.m. — the time when sunlight disappeared and the sundial became useless in that year.
Key Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 The date on the painting "15. 4. 2" uses "2" rather than the correct format "Year II" or "1794" ~30% (when the painting is discovered) Lupin later points out: people of that era would not mix writing formats this way; "2" must have another meaning
2 The madman Charles arrives at the garden at the same moment each year on April 15 and departs at the same moment (5:27 p.m.) ~50% (as recounted by the lawyer) The departure time is determined by the sun's position, emphasizing that "time" is the core dimension for solving the puzzle
3 Upon arriving, Lupin notices the sculptural form of the sundial and the direction where the sun at 2 p.m. falls on the crack in the surface ~92% Action precedes explanation: readers see Lupin pry open the crack with a knife, but do not yet understand why
4 18 diamonds are extracted one by one from the crack ~94% Final confirmation
Supporting Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 Before d'Ernemont was arrested, he briefly escaped into the garden and only came out calmly to surrender "6 to 7 minutes later" ~50% Those 6 to 7 minutes were enough to hide 18 diamonds — but knowing the crack is required to understand the intention
2 The property was never found; three "paintings" were the dying words revealed by the old maidservant ~50% Three paintings = three messages: corresponding to wife, son, and daughter; the sundial is the shared core of the clues
3 Sunlight, the sundial, and shadows appear as central objects in all three paintings ~30% "The sign of the shadow" is the title itself: the sun and shadow are the carriers of the cipher
Red Herrings
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Misleading Direction
1 A century of digging by others (turning soil, probing wells, chipping walls) ~50–70% Leads readers to also "search downward" rather than "look up at the sundial"
2 The lawyer's belief that the treasure does not exist and is merely the old maidservant's fantasy ~65% Lowers the reader's expectations of the puzzle, making the revelation all the more stunning

Story 9: "The Invisible Prisoner"

Mystery Type: Hiding-place puzzle (Where is he hiding?)

Convergence Points by Aspect: - The elderly vagrant's hiding place (the scarecrow): approximately 93% - The mystery of drinking water (three connected wheat stalks): approximately 90% - Average convergence point: 91% → ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (extreme difficulty: the answer is nearly impossible to deduce before the revelation)

Truth: The Vagrant Disguised as a Scarecrow Hidden in a Tree

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 93%
  • Answer: Trainard seized the moment after the farm boy shot the scarecrow down, slipped on the straw costume in the confusion, climbed back up the original tree, and spent an entire month lying motionless on a branch in the posture of a scarecrow. At night he used three connected wheat stalks extending into the water trough to drink from a distance like a straw in a milkshake, and was never detected by the night watchmen.
Key Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 The scarecrow is initially shot down by the eldest farm son, falls onto a large branch and hangs there, and is not removed ~3% The scarecrow remains at the scene in a "knocked down and hanging from a branch" posture — readers notice this but do not ask "what happened to it afterward?"
2 The farmer says "that scarecrow really irritates me, always seeing that stupid thing from the window at mealtimes" ~5% This remark implies the scarecrow has been hanging there all along — but whether it is the original scarecrow or a replacement, no one questions
3 After Lupin's visit, three connected wheat stalks are found near the water trough ~90% "Drinking from such a distance" — Lupin immediately understands and fires toward the tree
4 A shot rings out; the "scarecrow" falls from the tree and scrambles to escape ~93% Final confirmation
Supporting Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 The farm's walls are extremely high and impossible to climb over; days of searching yield nothing Throughout Confirms that Trainard must still be within the courtyard, not having escaped
2 There is only one water source within the courtyard: the water trough ~40% If someone is here, they must approach the water source to drink — yet the night watchmen detected no movement
3 14 days of searching are conducted entirely at "ground level" ~40–85% Everyone looks at the ground; no one looks up
Red Herrings
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Misleading Direction
1 Farm residents believe he is hiding in some underground burrow or tree hollow ~25% Leads readers to envision hiding underground
2 The scarecrow falls and hangs from the tree right at the beginning ~5% Readers only remember "the scarecrow fell," not realizing it later "stood back up again"

Story 10: "Edith Swan-Neck"

Mystery Type: Identity deception + insurance fraud (Who is Sparmiento?)

Convergence Points by Aspect: - Colonel Sparmiento = Arsène Lupin: approximately 88% (when Inspector Ganimard explains to his superior) - "Edith" = Lupin's accomplice Sonia: approximately 93% - The "corpse" came from the Lille morgue: approximately 92% - Average convergence point: 91% → ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (extreme difficulty: the most complex identity puzzle in the entire collection)

Truth: The Colonel Is Lupin; the Entire Tragedy Is an Insurance Scheme

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 91%
  • Answer: "Colonel Sparmiento" is yet another of Lupin's disguises. He first steals one tapestry on a train to generate publicity, then as the Colonel insures "a complete set of 12" for 800,000 francs with great fanfare, elaborately installs security devices, hosts a party, and stages a full-cast alarm spectacle that evening — all of this is merely a distraction, making everyone believe "Lupin broke in," while the Colonel himself quietly passes the tapestries out through a second-floor window, then substitutes a corpse to fake his own death, leaves behind a note suggesting suicide from despair, and allows his wife (actually Lupin's former lover Sonia) to collect the 800,000 franc insurance payout in installments as an "innocent widow."
Key Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 In a letter after stealing one tapestry, Lupin threatens "next time I will take all twelve" — and this is precisely what later happens ~5% If Lupin "steals" all twelve, while he is simultaneously the Colonel who is "robbed," this contradiction, if caught, can lead to deducing his identity
2 The various security devices the Colonel installs are abnormally elaborate to the point that "only he knows the location of each alarm" ~20% A person genuinely worried about theft has no need to keep security device locations secret even from the insurance company — unless he needs to be able to disable them himself
3 The Inspector notes: Lupin "would never let anyone die because of his actions" — the Colonel's death is therefore suspicious ~82% Ganimard's core logic: if Lupin did it, the Colonel should not truly be dead
4 An unidentified corpse at the Lille morgue mysteriously disappears the night of the incident ~90% (Ganimard discovers this in newspaper clippings) This body was transported to Vildavraye, dressed in the Colonel's clothes, and placed beside a railway track
5 The "butler" (servant) is called "my darling" by Lupin's nanny Victoire — Ganimard recognizes the phrase ~95% Final confirmation: this "servant" is Lupin himself
Supporting Clues
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Description
1 The Colonel's reaction after the tapestry theft is "unusually composed" — he even hurries out to "attend an important appointment" ~65% A person who has lost a priceless treasure should not be this calm
2 Edith (the "widow") corresponds perfectly in appearance to the Edith Swan-Neck image from the tapestry ~12% This is the author's planted visual correspondence — but before the ending, readers will only appreciate the metaphor, not knowing it is a carefully designed deception
3 Both the Colonel's study and bedroom have windows facing Rue Dufresnoy, yet those windows "have been bricked up" ~15% The "bricked up" window becomes an unmonitored exit — this is where the tapestries were removed
Red Herrings
# Clue Content Appearance Point (%) Misleading Direction
1 The alarm spectacle (all lights suddenly extinguished, alarm bells ringing) creates the illusion of "Lupin invading" ~40% Causes everyone (including readers) to assume this is a standard Lupin smokescreen, rather than interference created by the Colonel himself
2 Three private detectives posted in the building every night ~20–60% Reinforces the "external intrusion" framework, distracting from the possibility of "an inside job"
3 "Edith's" pale, grief-stricken, and sympathetic appearance Throughout the second half The perfect innocent victim image causes both the insurance company and readers to lower their guard

Reasoning Path Reconstruction

Per-Story Path Summary

Story 1 "Two Hundred Thousand Francs Reward!" — Reasoning Path:

A careful reader should notice at 6% (after the code translation is complete) that "these spelling errors could not have been made by the secretary" — this is the earliest substantive clue available for independent deduction in the entire book. Decoding ETNA is not particularly difficult; it only requires cross-referencing the context ("the Baron's racehorse").

The true challenge is deducing "the Baron is the killer." Even before 72% (Lupin's accusation), readers have very limited circumstantial evidence to work with: the tiepin detail only appears at 60%, and "the Baroness's body is in the safe" has no early hints whatsoever. Leblanc constructs the Baron as a perfect victim, and only when Lupin launches his sudden accusation are readers pulled into the shock of "ah, so that's it."

Story 3 "The Sign of the Shadow" — Reasoning Path:

The critical breakthrough is "the meaning of 2." Readers sufficiently familiar with the French Revolutionary calendar might feel uneasy at 30% — but they must further realize that if this "2" represents a year, the writing format should be different, before they can advance to the conclusion of "2 o'clock." Leaping from "2 o'clock" to "on April 15 at 2 p.m., the sundial's shadow falls on the crack" requires multiple logical leaps, making it practically impossible to complete before the revelation.

Story 9 "The Invisible Prisoner" — Reasoning Path:

The "scarecrow" appears in a comedic fashion at the story's opening (shot down, hanging from a tree), but thereafter completely vanishes from the reader's awareness. "The three connected wheat stalks by the water trough" is the only effective clue, and it only appears after 90% of the story. Any "deduction" before this point can only be guesswork, not reasoning based on evidence.

Story 10 "Edith Swan-Neck" — Reasoning Path:

The most difficult reasoning in the entire book. Lupin's threat to "steal all twelve" (5%) and the Colonel's "soundproofed second layer of windows" (15%) could theoretically cause readers to grow suspicious, but leaping from "the Colonel might be problematic" to "the Colonel is Lupin" requires accepting a premise that is nearly absurd. Ganimard himself says: "This is not Lupin's style — getting someone killed over this." This moral logic (Lupin would not cause someone's death) is actually the key to solving the case, not a physical clue.

Additional Notes

The Confessions in the Context of Leblanc's Works

This book is the collection in the Lupin series that most fully showcases the "Lupin as detective" dimension. In the early series, Lupin appeared primarily as a thief, with detectives as his adversaries (Ganimard, Holmes). By the time of the Confessions, Leblanc boldly makes Lupin the solver of mysteries — not only breaking external puzzles, but packaging his own criminal acts as puzzles for readers to experience.

Leblanc's Technique of "Information Control"

Compared to Poe and Conan Doyle, Leblanc's detective fiction design has one fundamental distinction: he habitually has Lupin conceal his reasoning process.

  • Poe's Dupin displays the chain of reasoning (the step-by-step deduction in "The Purloined Letter").
  • Doyle's Holmes progressively explains clues before the revelation ("pray observe this mud...").
  • Leblanc's Lupin typically offers a retrospective explanation only at the ending: "Oh, I knew all along — would you like to hear how?"

This "meta-explanatory" mode of narration makes it fundamentally impossible for readers to reason in sync during the reading experience; they can only admire Lupin's brilliance at the end. From the perspective of detective literature, this represents a choice in which the suspense entertainment value takes precedence, with competitive fairness as a secondary concern.

The Special Tribute in Story 7

"Shadowy Love" (Story 7) explicitly references Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue": "a non-human killer." Lupin himself acknowledges within the story that this puzzle is merely a "trifle" for him, since Poe had already established the prototype. Leblanc thereby demonstrates a high degree of self-awareness: he knows that his puzzle designs are built upon the shoulders of giants who came before, and that Lupin is not Holmes, but rather the person who "knows more about the case than Holmes but deliberately conceals it."

Cultural Notes for Taiwanese Readers

"The Sign of the Shadow" (Story 3) involves substantial background on French Revolutionary history (the fate of "tax collectors" during the Revolution, the use of the Republican Calendar) and the geography of the Passy district of Paris. The Lupin series is generally rich in details of French aristocratic culture, and "Lupin's Marriage" (Story 8) in the Confessions takes particular delight in satirizing the arrogance of the old nobility, echoing Leblanc's critical perspective on social class — Lupin can marry a duke's daughter precisely because the aristocracy is so entrenched in tradition that their own security perimeters collapse.
