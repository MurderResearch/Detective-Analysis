The Blonde Lady — Detective Analysis

Basic Information

  • Author: Maurice Leblanc
  • Year: 1907 (French original, Arsène Lupin contre Herlock Sholmès); English translation published 1910
  • Type: Novel (comprising two independent story arcs)
  • Detective: Holmlock Shears (a thinly veiled parody of Sherlock Holmes), assisted by French inspector Ganimard
  • Core mystery types: whodunit × howdunit × wherefound — three mystery types layered upon one another

Synopsis

Part One: The Blonde Lady

Everything begins with a lottery ticket. Gerbois, a mathematics professor in Versailles, happens to purchase a ticket with a winning number — only to find both the ticket and its record stolen from his desk. The thief promptly reveals himself: Arsène Lupin. The million-franc ticket becomes the naked bargaining chip between the two men; Lupin takes Gerbois's daughter Suzanne hostage to force the professor's hand, ultimately splitting five hundred thousand francs with him.

Around the same time, a murder shocks Paris on the Avenue Henri-Martin: the retired diplomat Baron d'Hautrec is found dead in his bedroom, the rare jewel known as the Blue Diamond (a former French crown jewel) still on his finger, the killer nowhere to be found. Investigation reveals that a female companion called "Antoinette Bréhat" has vanished — and her hair color matches exactly the golden-haired woman witnessed in the Gerbois affair.

Then comes a curious episode at an auction: a wealthy bidder named Herr Bleichen is on the verge of outbidding everyone for the Blue Diamond when a mysterious note distracts him at the decisive moment, allowing the Countess de Crozon to acquire the gem. Six months later, the Blue Diamond is stolen again from the Countess's summer villa — only to be "recovered" in the tooth-powder jar belonging to the Austrian consul Bleichen.

At this point, the celebrated London detective Holmlock Shears (Leblanc's comic stand-in for Holmes) is commissioned to intervene, and the great duel begins. Shears follows a crucial lead: three crime scenes — the lawyer Detinan's apartment on the Rue Clapeyron, the Baron's townhouse at 134 Avenue Henri-Martin, and the Château de Crozon — were all designed and built by the same architect, Lucien Destange. Hidden passageways allowed the Blonde Lady to appear and vanish at will. Shears disguises himself as a new secretary to infiltrate the Destange residence, and ultimately confirms that "the Blonde Lady" is none other than the supposedly homebound, dark-haired daughter — Clotilde Destange. She is Lupin's lover, and for his sake she has committed murder and theft, concealing the torment of her conscience behind tear-filled eyes.

Shears attempts to use Clotilde as leverage, but in the very midst of their conversation she has already alerted Lupin under the guise of telephoning her dressmaker — and the "taxi driver" outside is Lupin himself. Shears is forcibly driven to the Normandy coast and expelled aboard a steam yacht. Lupin is eventually cornered in a hideout on the Rue Chalgrin by Shears and Ganimard, yet escapes via a concealed elevator. Shears trades Clotilde's freedom for the real Blue Diamond, which has been hidden inside a hollow sphere at the top of Lupin's walking stick.

Part Two: The Jewish Lamp

Shears returns from London to Paris once more, this time to investigate what appears to be a break-in at the home of Baron d'Ambremésy: someone has shattered a window and stolen an antique Jewish lamp — whose base conceals a priceless gold chimera set with rubies and jade. Shears immediately concludes this is an inside job; the ladder marks and glass-cutting outside are an elaborate fabrication.

The governess Alice Demun becomes the prime suspect. Shears discovers cut-out letters in the children's alphabet book and reassembles them into a telegraph code revealing secret communications between Alice and "Lupin" through the Echo de France. Alice eventually confesses — but when the Baron remarks on a single detail (the study door had been bolted from the inside), Shears abruptly realizes Alice is innocent. The true culprit is the Baroness, Suzanne d'Ambremésy: she had written private letters that fell into the hands of a blackmailer named Bresson, and had secretly engaged Alice to contact Lupin, offering the theft of the Jewish lamp in exchange for the return of those letters. Alice, out of deep loyalty to her mistress, willingly took the blame. Bresson ultimately kills himself as Shears and Ganimard close in; the treasures sink into the Seine, from which Shears eventually recovers them.

Truth Dissected

Part One: The Blonde Lady

Truth One: The True Identity of "the Blonde Lady"

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 75%
  • Answer: Clotilde Destange, daughter of the celebrated architect Lucien Destange and Lupin's secret lover. She appears under three aliases: "Antoinette Bréhat," the Baron's female companion; "Madame de Réal," who recommends the Blue Diamond to the Countess; and the mysterious "Blonde Lady" who escorts Lupin out of Detinan's office. She appears with brilliantly golden hair and afterward reverts to her natural dark coloring.

Truth Two: The True Whereabouts of the Blue Diamond

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 93%
  • Answer: The Blue Diamond found in Bleichen's tooth-powder jar is a replica (a counterfeit produced by remelting powdered diamond under high heat). The real Blue Diamond, ever since the Blonde Lady "failed" to remove it from the Baron's finger, has been in Lupin's possession all along. He hid the genuine stone inside the hollow rotating ball at the top of his walking stick. Shears ultimately exchanges Clotilde's freedom for the real diamond.

Truth Three: How Lupin Vanishes from a Locked Room — The Architect's Secret Passages

  • Clue convergence point: approximately 65%
  • Answer: When architect Lucien Destange designed the three buildings, he secretly incorporated hidden passages, concealed staircases, or sliding wall panels connecting them to adjacent structures. Over the years, Lupin — operating under the alias "Maxime Bermond" — assisted Destange with late-stage construction work on old projects, thereby gaining complete knowledge of every passage and exploiting them to his advantage.

Part Two: The Jewish Lamp

Truth One: The Truth of the Jewish Lamp Theft — Not an Outside Job, but a Web of Debt and Emotion

  • Clue convergence point (surface level — the external intrusion is false): ~15%; deep level (the true mastermind): ~95%

  • Surface illusion: The ladder, the glass cutting, the holes in the ground — all fabricated. Shears sees through the pretense within an hour of arriving.
  • Deeper truth: Baroness Suzanne d'Ambremésy had written private letters that fell into the hands of a blackmailer named Bresson. To recover the letters, she secretly engaged the governess Alice Demun to contact Lupin, offering the theft of the Jewish lamp (which concealed a jade-and-ruby gold chimera) as payment. Alice, in order to protect her mistress, ultimately volunteered to confess — but a single detail gave her away.

Reasoning Path Reconstruction

Part One: The Path to Solving the Blonde Lady Mystery

A path that Shears could follow — and that readers could theoretically follow as well:

The reader's first opportunity arises at approximately 25%: Ganimard's report notes that Antoinette Bréhat's hair "exactly matches" the Blonde Lady's. At this point, an attentive reader should recognize that the two names may well belong to the same person.

The second opportunity comes around 35%: Madame de Réal actively encourages the Countess to buy the Blue Diamond, then mysteriously "steals it back" from the Countess's own home — an identical pattern of behavior. "The Blonde Lady" has now left a unified pattern of action across three cases.

The true turning point that illuminates things for Shears comes at 65% — the discovery of the architect's inscription.

Prior to this, Shears had already noticed "Destange, Architect" on the lintels of 25 and 23 Rue Clapeyron (while Wilson's arm was being set, Shears alone spotted the inscription). He then hurries to the Avenue Henri-Martin to verify it, and confirms the Château de Crozon by telephone — three points connected in a line, a single common architect linking three spatially unrelated cases into one chain of cause and effect.

From this point on, Shears's path is entirely followable by the reader: - The architect → locating him in person → infiltrating his residence → discovering Lupin moving freely under an alias → finding Clotilde's secret room (connecting to an adjacent building) → confirming the Blonde Lady's identity.

It is worth noting that Leblanc immediately describes "Clotilde's silhouette" once Shears has infiltrated the household, giving the reader a clear visual cue. The one moment readers cannot quite "keep up" is how Shears "thought of" the architect's inscription so quickly — this intuitive leap arrives somewhat abruptly, and even Shears himself admits he cannot fully explain why the idea struck him at precisely the moment Wilson broke his arm.

Part Two: The Path to Solving the Jewish Lamp Mystery

The brilliance of this arc lies in its two-tiered truth:

First tier (approximately 15%): The fictitious external burglar Shears sees through the pretense in under an hour, and this path is entirely open to the reader. Measuring the discrepancy between the holes (9 inches below the balcony, 11 inches from the railing, identical in shape yet non-parallel), retrieving the fake stake from the garden — these are all "visible clues" the reader could in principle discover. Shears's deduction is: the real thief knew the precise location of each object, meaning he had lived or worked there.

Second tier (approximately 95%): Who is the true mastermind Alice's confession momentarily relaxes both reader and Shears alike. Then an unremarkable remark by the Baron — "the study door was bolted from the inside" — punctures Alice's lie at almost the very last instant.

The logic of this detail is not difficult to grasp: if Alice stole the lamp from the study, the study door must have been opened from inside by someone already in the room. And at that hour, only the Baron and Baroness were in the study. So one of them let Alice in — or alternatively, one of them stole the lamp and handed it to Alice to carry out.

When Shears turns to look at the Baroness, the expression on her face says everything.

This "reversal" is not beyond the reader's reach, but it requires maintaining vigilance about the "bolted door detail" even after hearing Alice's apparently complete confession. Leblanc plays a trick of "information overload" here: Alice's seemingly perfect admission smothers the one and only logical flaw.

Additional Notes

The Special Place of The Blonde Lady in the Arsène Lupin Series

The Blonde Lady (known in English as Arsène Lupin versus Holmlock Shears) marks a decisive turning point in Leblanc's series: Lupin is no longer merely a gentleman thief outwitting bumbling policemen — he faces a worthy adversary head-on.

Leblanc's "Shears" is obviously a direct borrowing from Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes — the name is altered by only a few letters, "Wilson" stands in for "Watson," and "Shears's 219 Park Lane" echoes "221B Baker Street." This kind of intertextual mockery is a literary game in itself: Leblanc is taunting Conan Doyle while announcing to readers that Lupin can defeat England's cleverest detective.

Yet interestingly, Leblanc does not hand Lupin a one-sided victory. Shears is toyed with at almost every turn, yet never truly defeated — he uncovers the architect's clue, confirms the Blonde Lady's identity, recovers the real Blue Diamond, and ultimately succeeds in having Lupin arrested in the second arc (though Lupin escapes by his customarily theatrical means). This design of "two evenly matched opponents trading wins and losses" is one of the most compelling qualities of The Blonde Lady.

Structural Features of the Mystery Design

This book is not a traditional detective novel — it is a work of literary pursuit and gamesmanship.

In a conventional detective story, the reader's pleasure comes from following the detective toward a solution. In this book, the reader simultaneously follows three perspectives — Shears (the pursuer), Lupin (the fugitive who counters with pursuit of his own), and an omniscient authorial narrator (maintaining a tone of bemused, appreciative neutrality). This triangular viewpoint means the "mystery" is not merely "who is the culprit," but "who will outmaneuver whom next."

Several of Leblanc's design choices are worth noting:

  1. The "false capture" double structure: In Part One, Shears believes he has caught the Blonde Lady (he has not), and believes Lupin has been cornered by Ganimard (he has, but Lupin still escapes). This rhythm of "just caught him — lost him again" deliberately oscillates readers between "nearly won" and "lost once more," generating a sustained readerly tension.

  2. The deliberate dislocation of the mystery's pace: The book's central mystery (the architect's secret passages) is not unlocked by Shears until two-thirds of the way through — but once revealed, his actions become extremely rapid, compressing the gap between "knowing the key fact" and "approaching resolution" into a very short space, giving the second half a sprinting, headlong momentum.

  3. Love as the vessel of crime: Clotilde commits murder and theft for the sake of her love for Lupin, yet maintains an almost religious serenity and composure throughout. In Part Two, Alice sacrifices herself for her mistress out of devoted friendship. Both women's "willing self-sacrifice" makes the traditional act of "unmasking the culprit" seem cruel and morally complicated — and Shears ultimately chooses to "turn a blind eye" to both of them, through which Leblanc questions whether "justice" means exposing the truth or destroying something deeply human.

Comparison with Other Works in the Series

Compared with Leblanc's shorter Lupin stories, The Blonde Lady operates at a much higher density of mystery: the short stories typically pursue a single thread, whereas this book simultaneously manages three (the Blonde Lady's identity, the Blue Diamond's location, and the architect's secret) and then adds a more intricate "double reversal" in the second arc.

Compared with Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes, Shears's thought process is described in a more externalized fashion (he often speaks his deductions aloud), making him slightly easier for readers to follow. Yet Lupin's presence means Shears is perpetually being watched and countered, undermining the traditional detective's aura of omniscience, so that readers — while identifying with the detective — cannot help their pulse quickening for that smiling man walking a tightrope.

A Note for English-Language Readers

"Holmlock Shears" is Leblanc's parody of Sherlock Holmes, with the name slightly rearranged. The original French edition used "Herlock Sholmès" — even more transparent. Earlier English translations occasionally rendered the character directly as "Sherlock Holmes," but strictly speaking the two characters are legally distinct: Conan Doyle raised objections to Leblanc during his lifetime, which prompted Leblanc to alter the name to "Holmlock Shears" in later French editions.

The "Jewish Lamp" (Lampe Juive in French) refers to the form of the traditional Hanukkah oil lamp common in Hebrew tradition, which was highly prized in the late-nineteenth-century European antiques market. Beyond its practical function as a hiding place for valuables, Leblanc's choice of this object carries the romantic projection of French society at the time onto an "exotic, oriental" sense of mystery.

The backstory of the "Blue Diamond" alludes to the historical "French Blue" — a stone once belonging to Louis XIV that later became the Hope Diamond. Leblanc cleverly draws on this real historical legend to lend his fictional treasure a measure of genuine weight.
