The Submarine Plans — Detective Analysis

Basic Information

  • Author: Agatha Christie
  • Year: 1923 (first published in The Sketch magazine; later collected in The Under Dog and Other Stories, 1951)
  • Type: Short Story
  • Detective: Hercule Poirot (赫丘勒·白羅), Hastings (海斯汀)
  • Core Mystery Type: whodunit (identity of the thief) + twist (fake plans twist)

Overall Deducibility Rating

Metric Rating Notes
Deducibility Score ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Average Clue Convergence Point at approximately 80% — the thief's true identity is logically deducible at the 75% mark, while the fake-plans twist cannot be confirmed until around 90%
Fog Rating ⭐⭐⭐ Multiple Red Herrings intertwine: a phantom intruder, the French maid's "ghost" scream, and a genuine spy among the guests — the misdirections pull in competing directions, though the overall density of obfuscation is moderate

Synopsis

Late at night, Poirot and Hastings are summoned to the country estate of Lord Alloway (奧洛伊勳爵) at Sharples (夏普爾斯). Alloway, the Minister of Defence on the cusp of becoming Prime Minister, is in a confidential meeting with Admiral Sir Harry Weardale (韋爾代爾爵士), the First Sea Lord, to discuss a top secret matter: the design plans for the new Type-Z submarine.

That very night, the plans vanish during a three-minute window when the secretary Fitzroy (菲茨羅伊) briefly steps away from the study. Suspicion falls upon the weekend guests: Lady Weardale, her rakish son Leonard Weardale (萊昂納多), and a beautiful widow of obscure origin who moves in diplomatic circles, Mrs. Conrad (康拉德夫人).

In the course of his investigation, Poirot methodically rules out an outside intruder (no footprints on the terrace), the French maid's ghost story (in fact, Leonard had stolen a kiss from her in the dark), and even Leonard himself (he was on the staircase kissing the maid at the relevant time, giving him a solid alibi). Lady Alloway quietly implores Poirot to let the matter rest, hinting that she suspects her own son is the thief.

In the end, Poirot's cool logic leads him to a shocking truth: Lord Alloway himself is the thief. Under the pressure of blackmail, he "provided" the plans to the spy Mrs. Conrad — but he had quietly tampered with the technical specifications in advance, rendering the documents effectively useless. The "theft" was a scheme of his own devising: a performance that allowed him to satisfy the blackmailer while safeguarding Britain's real secrets.

The story closes with a brief coda: a foreign power builds a submarine from the plans, with catastrophic results. Alloway later becomes Prime Minister and sends Poirot a personally signed photograph — inscribed "To my discreet friend."

Truth Breakdown

First Truth: The Identity of the Plans Thief (whodunit)

  • Clue Convergence Point: approximately 75%
  • Answer: Lord Alloway himself. Under the cover of "handing over the plans to the other side," he personally removed the document from his own desk.

Key Clues

# Clue Content Story Position (%) Notes
1 Poirot inspects the terrace and confirms that the rain-soaked lawn bears no footprints whatsoever, ruling out an outside intruder ~33% Eliminates the possibility of an outside party, narrowing suspicion to those inside the house
2 The French maid confesses: she cried out because Leonard had kissed her in the night, not because she had seen a ghost ~57% Exposes the "ghost" Red Herring and simultaneously establishes Leonard's alibi — he was on the staircase at the time and could not have entered the study to steal the plans
3 Alloway himself insists that Fitzroy could not possibly be involved: "He could have copied them at any convenient moment" ~30% This seemingly persuasive defence is in fact self-defeating logic: if the person with the greatest opportunity (the secretary) has no motive to steal, then who would have reason to make a "theft" appear to have occurred?
4 After eliminating all other suspects, Poirot reasons in the car: while the plans lay on the desk, only Alloway entered the study; if Fitzroy did not take them, they must have been taken by the Lord himself ~75% The final conclusion of the elimination method — at this point the reader possesses sufficient information to arrive at the same deduction independently

Supporting Clues

# Clue Content Story Position (%) Notes
1 Hastings mentions that Lord Alloway was once touched by a "stock manipulation" scandal, which was later cleared up ~10% Plants a background suggestion that the Lord is not entirely without blemish
2 Alloway eagerly urges Poirot "not to suspect Fitzroy," displaying unusual insistence ~30% If he had no ulterior motive, why would the host be so anxious to defend his secretary?
3 Poirot offers Alloway an extravagant compliment ("England is fortunate to have you"), delivered in a subtly curious tone ~62% Poirot's flattery tends to carry strategic intent; here it suggests he has already formed a conclusion and is managing the suspect

Red Herrings

# Clue Content Story Position (%) Misdirection
1 Alloway claims he saw "a shadow" slipping out of the study from the terrace ~18% Creates the impression of an outside intruder, sending the investigation outward
2 Mrs. Conrad — a suspicious "fixture in diplomatic circles" with a mysterious private life, under surveillance by intelligence services Throughout She is indeed a contact for a foreign spy network, but in this case she is the recipient, not the thief; Christie deliberately makes her appear to be the most likely culprit
3 Leonard is found away from his room late at night with no clear explanation ~50% Leads the reader to suspect this "dissolute young man" may be involved; Lady Alloway herself mistakes her son for the thief for the same reason
4 The French maid's "ghost" shriek — causing chaos and prompting Fitzroy to leave the study ~20% Suggests that an external accomplice exploited the commotion; in reality it is an entirely unrelated chance event

Second Truth: The Fake-Plans Twist (howdunit / deeper motivation)

  • Clue Convergence Point: approximately 88%
  • Answer: Alloway is not truly a traitor. Drawing on his engineering expertise, he introduced undetectable technical errors into the plans he handed over, making them worthless in practice. He staged the "theft" as a performance: by delivering this defective forgery to the blackmailer, he freed himself from the blackmail while protecting Britain's genuine secrets.

Key Clues

# Clue Content Story Position (%) Notes
1 Lord Alloway's background: he began his career as an industrial engineer and "entered the House of Commons as the head of a large manufacturing concern" ~8% Establishes his professional credentials — he has both the knowledge to identify and the skill to alter technical drawings
2 Poirot reasons: "A man who has genuinely handed over the real plans would not be so concerned about whether suspicion falls on Fitzroy" ~82% If Alloway were a straightforward traitor, he would have no reason to take such care protecting his secretary — his concern reveals that he needs the whole affair to appear plausible
3 Before leaving, Poirot tells Alloway in private, "I have confidence in you," implying he understands the scheme and has chosen to let it stand ~84% Poirot is not condoning treason; he has concluded that the plans have already been "defused"
4 Alloway says: "I believe in myself." In context, the remark carries a double meaning ~85% It is not merely a statesman's declaration of self-assurance — it is also an engineer's confidence in his own capacity to "modify" the plans
5 The closing confirmation: a foreign power builds a submarine from the plans, and "the result was a dismal failure" ~97% The coda retrospectively validates that the fake-plans strategy succeeded

Supporting Clues

2 Alloway insists "he does not wish this matter made public" and is unwilling to pursue any conditions for the return of the plans ~65% What he truly needs is not the recovery of the real document, but for the whole affair to pass by quietly

Reasoning Path Reconstruction

First Path: Who is the Thief?

By around the 33% mark (no footprints on the terrace), an attentive reader should have eliminated the outside intruder and begun turning their gaze inward. The French maid's confession at 55–60% reveals Leonard's alibi and simultaneously destroys the assumption of an outside accomplice.

At this point the logic of the case comes into clear focus: Fitzroy had ample opportunity to copy the plans at leisure and thus no reason to risk an obvious theft; Leonard was not near the study; Mrs. Conrad's room is upstairs, making it impossible for her to slip in and out of the study undetected in so short a time. The only remaining "impossible" conclusion is that the plans never left Lord Alloway's sight — they went directly into his own pocket.

By the time Poirot reasons aloud in the car at the 75% mark, any reader who has diligently tracked the elimination process should be capable of reaching the same conclusion independently.

Second Path: The Fake-Plans Twist

This path runs considerably deeper. The engineering background (8%) is the only capability clue planted in advance; all remaining hints are buried in Poirot's behaviour — the excessive praise, the conspicuously omitted follow-up questions, the parting words "I have confidence in you." For readers who miss these details, the fake-plans twist will arrive as a sudden surprise only at the closing coda at 97%. For readers who have been carefully tracking the nuances of Poirot's attitude, a sense that something more than straightforward treason is afoot becomes perceptible at around 82–85%, though the precise mechanism remains difficult to confirm.

Additional Notes

Christie's Fondness for the "Clever Good Man"

Among Christie's short stories, The Submarine Plans belongs to a comparatively rare type: the genuine culprit is not a morally wicked person but a clever individual walking a tightrope under duress. Lord Alloway is a victim of blackmail — already a compromised position — yet when cornered with no avenue of escape, he devises an intricate scheme to let the enemy steal a worthless document. Christie gives him the punishment a reader might expect (his scheme is seen through by Poirot), while also extending him clemency (Poirot chooses not to expose him).

This structure echoes Christie's sustained exploration of the "morally ambiguous grey zone." In this case Poirot plays not the role of "the detective who unmasks the crime," but rather "the arbiter who judges whether unmasking is warranted" — a parallel to the ethical logic Poirot employs in The Murder of Roger Ackroyd and Murder on the Orient Express.

The Distinctive Quality of the Reasoning Structure: Pure Elimination

This story is a near-perfect demonstration of reasoning by elimination alone. The author provides a closed set of suspects (everyone inside the house) and systematically removes each one with evidence, until only the one "who cannot possibly have done it" remains. This is an exact enactment of the principle of "eliminating suspects" enshrined in Knox's Ten Commandments — and it is precisely this purity that gives the story exceptional instructional value among Christie's short works.

Contrast with "The Disappearance of Mr. Davenheim"

Both are puzzles of "something vanishing from a sealed environment in a short span of time" (in Davenheim it is a person; here it is a document), yet the methods are entirely different. The solution to Davenheim is a faked death as a means of disappearance; the solution here is that the thief and the "victim" are one and the same person. Read together, the two stories make a superb pair of illustrations of how many different solutions Christie can wring from a single "disappearance puzzle."

Hastings's Function

In this story Hastings's role is particularly representative: he instinctively dislikes Leonard ("he's a thorough rotter") and freely admits he never seriously suspected Fitzroy. This "reader surrogate's" failure of perception is a deliberate design choice that makes Poirot's final deduction stand out all the more sharply. Through Hastings's subjective bias, Christie deliberately engineers an "observer's blind spot," which Poirot's "little grey cells" then proceed to correct, one by one.
